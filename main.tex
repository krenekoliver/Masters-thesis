\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\noindent
In this work, we will first focus on the eigenvalue computations of the regular Sturm--Liouville problems. Two concrete examples are Paine and Coffey--Evans equations for which we will use several different methods for the discretization of the operator of the second derivative. We will compute our data and compare it to one another.

Next, we will study the classical wave equation and test Finite Differences and Fast Fourier Transform on their propagation.

Finally, we will focus on rate-type models for metamaterials. Our goal will be to use them for simulations of wave propagation.

Metamaterials are, by definition, “A synthetic composite material engineered to display properties not usually found in natural materials.”

These exotic properties are usually related to wave propagation phenomena in metamaterials, and they typically stem from a carefully designed microstructure.  

The thesis will focus on a particularly promising class of models (rate-type models) for metamaterials behavior. Both qualitative (refinement of models, qualitative features) and quantitative (numerical simulations) analyzes are needed.
	

\chapter{Theoretical framework}
\noindent
In this chapter, we will describe the core notions that will be used in the rest of this thesis. Concepts such as metamaterials, spectral methods, and rate-type models.

\section{Metamaterials}
\noindent
Metamaterials are artificially engineered composite structures designed to exhibit extraordinary physical properties not typically found in natural materials.

Examples of such properties include a negative refractive index, negative effective mass density, or negative bulk modulus, which enable phenomena like wave cloaking, superlensing, and band gap formation for controlling electromagnetic, acoustic, and elastic waves.

These properties arise from the precise arrangement of subwavelength unit cells or microstructures, rather than the intrinsic characteristics of the constituent materials, allowing for tailored responses to external stimuli through geometric design and material selection.



\section{Spectral methods}
\noindent
Spectral methods represent a class of numerical techniques for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) and other mathematical problems, characterized by their use of global basis functions to approximate solutions. Spectral methods expand the solution as a series of orthogonal polynomials or trigonometric functions, such as Fourier series for periodic problems or Chebyshev polynomials for non-periodic domains.

This global approximation leads to high-order accuracy, often achieving exponential convergence for smooth functions, as the error decreases faster than any algebraic power of the number of basis functions.

The core idea is to transform the problem into the spectral space, where differentiation becomes a simple algebraic operation via differentiation matrices, and then solve the resulting system efficiently \cite{cit2}.

One of the key advantages of spectral methods lies in their efficiency for problems with smooth solutions, where they require fewer degrees of freedom compared to low-order methods to achieve comparable accuracy.

For instance, in the context of wave propagation or elastodynamics, spectral methods preserve essential wave properties, such as phase and group velocities, without introducing artificial dissipation or dispersion that plagues finite difference schemes. 

In applications to metamaterials and elastic wave modeling, spectral methods excel in capturing dispersive behaviors and band gaps inherent to these engineered structures. By leveraging Fourier transforms for periodic lattices or Chebyshev expansions for bounded domains, they accurately resolve high-frequency modes without the numerical artifacts that could distort physical interpretations. As illustrated in \cite{cit2}, examples like the one-dimensional wave equation solved via Fourier spectral methods highlight their ability to maintain energy conservation and spectral accuracy over long simulation times.

This makes spectral methods an ideal choice for theoretical and computational studies in applied mathematics and materials science, providing a robust framework for validating continuum models against discrete lattice systems.



\section{Rate-type models}
\noindent
Rate-type constitutive models, also known as rate-dependent constitutive relations, represent a class of material descriptions in continuum mechanics where the stress (or force) is expressed as a function of the rate of deformation, its time derivatives, and possibly the stress itself, rather than solely depending on the current deformation state as in classical hyperelasticity.

Originating from the works of Truesdell (1955) and Oldroyd (1950), these models incorporate objective time derivatives of the stress tensor to ensure frame-indifference, making them particularly suitable for capturing dynamic and viscoelastic behaviors in complex materials without violating fundamental physical principles like positive definite mass density.

In the context of metamaterials, as demonstrated in \cite{cit1}, rate-type relations avoid the unphysical notion of negative effective mass by embedding the effects of internal microstructures—such as local resonances in mass-in-mass systems—into the constitutive equation for stress $\sigma$, which includes second-order time derivatives of $\sigma$ coupled with spatial derivatives of displacement. This approach maintains Newton's second law in its standard form while accurately reproducing dispersive wave propagation and band gaps, providing a physically consistent framework for modeling elastic waves in engineered structures like those considered in this thesis.


\chapter{Eigenvalue computations}
\noindent
The aim of this chapter is, first, to introduce two regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue test problems: namely, the Paine equation, first introduced in \cite{paine} and the Coffey--Evans equation, first presented in \cite{pryce}.

Then, we will describe methods that discretize these continuous problems and allow us to solve them approximately (numerically). Mainly, we will focus on how to discretize the operator of the second derivative, which means we will assemble the differentiation matrix, substitute it for the continuous derivative, and then compute the eigenvalues of the discretized problem.

The methods that will be introduced are: Finite Differences, Numerov's, Discrete Fourier Transform, Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev Collocation, and Discrete Sine Transform. 

Our goal is to compare all of these methods and figure out which one is best. For our purposes, we need some reference values. These will be calculated using the Matslise application.

\section{Test problems}
\noindent
In this section, we will introduce two regular Sturm--Liouville test problems on which we will apply the discretization techniques. The general form of these problems is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_general_test_problem}
    -u''(x) + q(x) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \quad x \in [a, \, b] \, , \quad u(a) = u(b) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
Now, let's look in more detail at two specific cases of this problem.

\subsection{Paine equation}
\noindent
The first one has parameters
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        q(x) & = e^x \, , \\[2pt]
        a & = 0 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi \ . \\[2pt]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute them into the relation \ref{eq_general_test_problem}, the resulting Paine equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_SL}
    -u''(x) + e^x u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \quad x \in [0, \, \pi] \, , \quad u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}


\subsection{Coffey--Evans equation}
\noindent 
In the second case, the parameters are
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE_coeffs}
    \begin{split}
        q(x) & = \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} -2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \, , \\[2pt]
        a & = -\pi/2 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi/2 \, . \\[2pt]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute them into \ref{eq_general_test_problem}, we get the resulting Coffey--Evans equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE}
    \begin{split}
    -u''(x) + \big(\beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} -2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \big) u(x) & = \lambda  u(x) \, , \\
     u(-\pi/2) = u(\pi/2) = 0 \, , \quad x \in [-\pi/2 , \, & \pi/2] \, . \\
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
In the whole text, we will use the value of parameter $\beta = 30$.


\section{Matslise application}
\noindent
As we have mentioned before, to obtain highly accurate reference eigenvalues of our test problems, a MATLAB application named Matslise has been implemented. In this thesis, it serves as the reference solver for computing benchmark eigenvalues and for validating results obtained by other numerical methods.

Matslise is a MATLAB package for the numerical solution of regular and singular Sturm-–Liouville eigenvalue problems. It belongs to the class of shooting methods: it transforms the two-point boundary value problem into an initial-value problem by guessing a trial eigenvalue $\lambda$, integrating the differential equation from one boundary to the other, and iteratively adjusting $\lambda$ until the second boundary condition is satisfied. The key innovation of Matslise is that the integration step is performed using piecewise constant perturbation methods. On each small subinterval, the potential $q(x)$ is approximated by a constant value (for which the exact analytic solution is known), and higher-order perturbation correction terms are systematically added. \cite{ledoux}

This approach yields very high accuracy even for large (high-index) eigenvalues (in which we are particularly interested) with relatively few grid points. It all makes Matslise particularly efficient and robust for our test problems. \cite{matslise}



\section{Discretization methods}
\noindent
In this section, we will introduce the discretization methods that will be used for the computation of the eigenvalues of our test problems and later on for spatial discretization in the classical wave equation and the evolution equation for metamaterials.

Our aim is to discretize the continuous operator of the second derivative by using an approximation matrix
\begin{equation}
    D \approx \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial  x^2} \, .
\end{equation}
These matrices will be assembled using the methods below, and then we will use MATLAB's \texttt{eig} function for the computation of eigenvalues. These eigenvalues will then be compared across the methods, especially the high-index ones.

Our goal is to rewrite the main equation \ref{eq_general_test_problem} to the form
\begin{equation}
    \big( -D + E  \big) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
for the Paine equation, where
\begin{equation}
    E = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}\big) \,.
\end{equation}
And in the case of the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    Q = \texttt{diag}\big(-2 \beta \cos{(2 x_1)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_1)} \, , \dots, \\ 
    -2 \beta \cos{(2 x_N)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_N)} \big) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
to the form
\begin{equation}
    \big( -D + Q  \big) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, .
\end{equation} 

The differentiation matrices $D$ will be assembled using the following methods.

\subsection{Finite Difference method}
\noindent
The first numerical method on our list is the Finite Difference method (FD). We will be using the second-order central Finite Difference formula, which approximates the second derivative on a discrete grid for $h$ small enough with equation
\begin{equation}
    u''(x_i) \approx \frac{u(x_i - h) - 2 u(x_i) + u(x_i + h) }{h^2} = \big(D \mathbf{u} \big)_i \, , \quad i = 1, \dots, N \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^T$ and $u(x_i+h) = u(x_{i+1})$. Matrix $D$ is
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{bmatrix}
    - 2 & 1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & - 2 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & - 2  & \cdots & 0 \\ 
    \vdots  & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & -2 \\ 
\end{bmatrix} \, .
\end{equation}
The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_1} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_2} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_1}  
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $D$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $D_{i,i} = 2 / h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $D_{i,i+1} = D_{i+1,i} = -1 / h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $E = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N} \big)$
\State $A = -D + E$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$ 
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $h = \pi/(N+1)$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $D$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $D_{i,i}   = 2/h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $D_{i,i+1} = D_{i+1,i} = -1/h^2$  \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $q_i = \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x_i)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $Q = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N \big)$
\State $A = -D + Q$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Numerov's method}
\noindent
Numerov's method uses higher-order Finite Differences and approximates \ref{eq_general_test_problem} with equation
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    b_{i-1} u(x_{i-1}) + a_{i} u(x_i ) + b_{i+1} u(x_{i+1}) = \lambda h^2 \big(u(x_{i-1}) + 10 u(x_i) + u(x_{i+1}) \big) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
that holds for all $i = 1, \dots,N$, where $u(x_i+h) = u(x_{i+1})$ and the coefficients $a_i$ and $b_i$ are
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        a_i & = + 24 + 10 h^2 q(x_i) \, ,   \\
        b_i & = -12 + h^2 q(x_{i}) \, .  \\
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we denote the tridiagonal matrices $A$ and $B$ by
\begin{equation}
A = \begin{bmatrix}
    a_1 & b_1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    b_1 & a_2 & b_2  & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & b_2 & a_3  & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & a_N  \\
\end{bmatrix} ,
\label{}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B = h^2\begin{bmatrix}
    10 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & 10 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 10 & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 10  \\
\end{bmatrix} .
\label{}
\end{equation}
The whole approximation can be rewritten in matrix form
\begin{equation}
    A \mathbf{u} = \lambda B \mathbf{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)\big]^T$. The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation. Algorithms contain the basic Numerov's method and the Numerov's method with correction.\cite{numerov}


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Numerov's method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_Numerov_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\State $q_i = \exp(x_i)$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $A$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $A_{i,i} = 24 + 10 h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $A_{i,i+1} = A_{i+1,i} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $B$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $B_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $B_{i,i+1} = B_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$,$B$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12  s_k^2  /  \big(h^2  (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}} \leq \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}} \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12 s_k^2 / \big(h^2 (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}} \leq \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}} \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method}
\noindent
The Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method (CDM) is a high-order spectral collocation technique that approximates the derivatives of a function at specific collocation points using global polynomial interpolation. It will be implemented using MATLAB's chebfun package, utilizing the \texttt{chebpts} and \texttt{diffmat} functions.

Using this method, we first generate the Chebyshev points of the second kind via \texttt{chebpts} on the interval $[-1, \, 1]$. Then, we assemble the second order differentiation matrix $D_{\text{full}}$ via the \texttt{diffmat}.

For problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        u(0) = u(\pi) = & \; 0 \, ,  \quad \text{or} \\
        u(-\pi/2) = u(\pi/2) = & \; 0 \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
the first and last rows and columns are removed to obtain the reduced (interior) differentiation matrix $D_{\text{Cheb}}$. This way, we enforce these boundary conditions.

We find the Chebyshev interior points $t_{\text{int}}$ in a similar manner. Next, we transform these points from the interval $[-1, \, 1]$ to the interval $[0, \, \pi]$ or $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$ with an appropriate scaling factor, and we also scale the matrix $D_{\text{Cheb}}$ accordingly.

Finally, we assemble the matrices $E$, $Q$, $A$ and compute the eigenvalues of the matrix problem
\begin{equation}
    A\mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbf{u} \, , \quad \text{where} \; \, \mathbf{u} = [u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)]^T \, ,
\end{equation}
and where the matrix $A$ combines the scaled differentiation matrix $D$ with the potential term $E$ or $Q$.\cite{cdm}\cite{trefethen}

The implementation of the method can be seen in algorithms \ref{alg_5} and \ref{alg_6}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_5}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{Cheb}} = D_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \pi/2 \, (t_{\text{int},i} + 1)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $D = (2 / \pi)^2 D_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $E = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}\big)$
\State $A = -D + E$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix – Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_6}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{Cheb}} = D_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$ 
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \pi/2 \, \big( t_{\text{int},i}\big)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $Q = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N \big)$
\State $D = (2 / \pi)^2 D_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $A = -D + Q$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method}
\noindent
The Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method (MBCDM) is a high-order spectral collocation technique designed for the efficient solution of Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, particularly the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations.

The method employs the \emph{Kosloff--Tal-Ezer (KTE) mapping}
\begin{equation}
    g(s) = \frac{\arcsin(\alpha s)}{\arcsin(\alpha)} \, , \quad s \in [-1,1] \, ,
\end{equation}
with the parameter $\alpha \in (0,1)$ controlling the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries. As the parameter $\alpha$  approaches 1, the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries becomes more pronounced.

The physical coordinate $x$ is obtained as
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2} \big( g(s) + 1 \big)
\end{equation}
for the interval $[0, \pi]$ (Paine problem), or
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2} \, g(s)
\end{equation}
for the symmetric interval $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ (Coffey--Evans problem). This nonlinear mapping significantly improves the accuracy of the method for the two highly oscillatory test problems (the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations), especially when computing high-index eigenvalues.

In the computational domain $s \in [-1,1]$, the first and second derivatives are approximated using Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrices $D_1$ and $D_2$. These are transformed to the physical domain via the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx} = \frac{ds}{dx} \frac{d}{ds}, \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2} = \left( \frac{ds}{dx} \right)^2 \frac{d^2}{ds^2} + \frac{d^2 s}{dx^2} \frac{d}{ds} \, .
\end{equation}
After incorporating the potential term $q(x)$ as a diagonal matrix ($E$ for the exponential potential in the Paine problem and $Q$ for the trigonometric potential in the Coffey--Evans problem), the discrete operator takes the form
\begin{equation}
A = -D + \text{potential matrix} \, .
\end{equation}
Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed by removing the first and last rows and columns, yielding the reduced interior matrix $A_{\text{int}}$. The eigenvalues of $A_{\text{int}}$ approximate the continuous spectrum with spectral accuracy.\cite{cdm}

The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\alpha$
\State $s =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(s) = \arcsin(\alpha s) / \arcsin(\alpha)$ 
\State $x = (\pi/2) (g(s) + 1)$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(s) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha s)^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(s) = \alpha^3 s \big((1-\alpha^2 s^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $dx/ds = (\pi/2) g'(s)$
\State $d^2x/ds^2 = (\pi/2) g''(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $D_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $D_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $D = \texttt{diag}\big((dx/ds)^{-2}\big) D_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2x/ds^2)(dx/ds)^{-3}\big) D_1$
\State $E = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_{N+2}}\big)$
\State $A = -D + E$
\State $A_{\text{int}} = A[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$, $\alpha$
\State $s =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(s) = \arcsin(\alpha s) / \arcsin(\alpha)$ 
\State $x = (\pi/2) g(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(s) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha s)^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(s) = \alpha^3 s \big((1-\alpha^2 s^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $dx/ds = (\pi/2) g'(s)$
\State $d^2x/ds^2 = (\pi/2) g''(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $D_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $D_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $D = \texttt{diag}\big((dx/ds)^{-2}\big) D_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2x/ds^2)(dx/ds)^{-3}\big) D_1$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N+2$
\State $Q = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_{N+2}\big)$
\State $A = -D + Q$
\State $A_{\text{int}} = A[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}




\subsection{Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev Collocation method}
\noindent
The Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev Collocation method (LGCC) combines a spectral Galerkin projection with Chebyshev collocation to solve eigenvalue problems of the form \eqref{eq_general_test_problem}.

The method begins by mapping the physical interval to the standard domain $[-1, \, 1]$ using a linear transformation
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x - (b+a)}{b-a} \in [-1, \, 1] \, ,
\end{equation}
where $[a, \, b]$ is the original interval. For Paine problem with interval $[0, \, \pi]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x-\pi}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \frac{\pi}{2}(t+1) \, .
\end{equation}
And for Coffey--Evans problem with interval $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} t \, .
\end{equation}

The basis functions are chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(t) = L_k(t) - L_{k+2}(t) \, , \quad k = 0,1,\dots,m-1 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $L_k(t)$ denotes the $k$-th Legendre polynomial. These basis functions automatically satisfy the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The weak form of the differential eigenvalue problem is then projected onto this basis, leading to the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
    (S + \widehat{M})\mathbf{u} = \mu T \mathbf{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^T$. $S$ is the (diagonal) stiffness matrix, $T$ is the (penta-diagonal) mass matrix, and $\widehat{M}$ is the symmetrized potential matrix obtained via Chebyshev interpolation of the product $q(t)\phi_j(t)$, followed by the extraction of the Legendre coefficients. The eigenvalues $\mu_k$ are finally rescaled to the original problem via $\lambda_k = (2/\pi)^2 \mu_k$ (for the interval length $\pi$).

The method achieves high accuracy for smooth potentials, thanks to the spectral convergence of the Legendre and Chebyshev expansions, while the special choice of basis ensures the exact enforcement of the Dirichlet boundary conditions.\cite{legendre_galerkin}

The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev Collocation - Paine equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Stiffness matrix $S$ (diagonal):
\State \quad $S_{k, \, k} = 4 k + 6$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Mass matrix $T$ (penta-diagonal):
\State \quad $T_{k,k} = \dfrac{2}{2 k+1} + \dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\State \quad $T_{k, \, k+2} = T_{k+2, \, k} = -\dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-3$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $q(t) = \dfrac{\pi^2}{4} \exp\left( \dfrac{\pi}{2} (t + 1) \right)$
\State \quad $\phi_k(t) = L_{k}(t) - L_{k+2}(t)$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Matrix $M$ (Galerkin projection of potential):
\State \quad for $j = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad $f_j(t) = q(t) \phi_j(t)$
\State \quad \quad $I_{f_j} =$ \texttt{chebfun}($f_j$,$N+1$) 
\State \quad \quad \texttt{legcoeffs}($I_{f_j}$) $ \to c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$
\State \quad \quad for $i = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad \quad $M_{i,j} = \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+1} \, c_{i+1} - \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+5} \, c_{i+3} $ \quad (if $i+3 \leq N$)
\BlankLine
\State $\hat{M} = \texttt{triu}(M) + \texttt{triu}(M, \, 1)^T$
\State $A = S + \hat{M}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$,$T$) $ \to \mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_m $
\State$\lambda_k = (2/ \pi)^2 \mu_k$ \quad for $k = 1, \dots, m$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev Collocation - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k = 0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Stiffness matrix $S$ (diagonal):
\State \quad $S_{k,k} = 4 k + 6$  \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Mass matrix $T$ (penta-diagonal):
\State \quad $T_{k,k} = \dfrac{2}{2k+1} + \dfrac{2}{2k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\State \quad $T_{k,k+2} = T_{k+2,k} = -\dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-3$
\BlankLine
\State $q(t) = \dfrac{\pi^2}{4} \big(\beta^2 \sin^2(\pi t) - 2 \beta \cos(\pi t) \big)$
\State $\phi_k(t) = L_{k}(t) - L_{k+2}(t)$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Matrix $M$ (Galerkin projection of potential):
\State \quad for $j = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad $f_j(t) = q(t) \phi_j(t)$
\State \quad \quad $I_{f_j} =$ \texttt{chebfun}($f_j$,$N+1$) 
\State \quad \quad \texttt{legcoeffs}($I_{f_j}$) $ \to c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$
\State \quad \quad for $i = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad \quad $M_{i,j} = \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+1} \, c_{i+1} - \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+5} \, c_{i+3}$ \quad (if $i+3 \leq N$)
\BlankLine
\State $\hat{M} = \texttt{triu}(M) + \texttt{triu}(M,1)^T$
\State $A = S + \hat{M}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$,$T$) $ \to \mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_m $
\State$\lambda_k = (2/ \pi)^2 \mu_k$ \quad for $k = 1, \dots, m$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Discrete Sine Transform}
\noindent
The Discrete Sine Transform (DST) provides a natural basis for solving Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a finite interval. 

In the DST spectral domain, the second derivative operator becomes a product with a matrix that has the negative squared wave numbers on the diagonal. Altogether, for a vector $\mathbf{y} = [y_1, \dots, y_N ]^T$ of interior grid values, the discrete Laplacian is realized by
\begin{equation}
    (\Delta_h \mathbf{y})_j = \mathrm{idst}\bigl(k \odot \mathrm{dst}(\mathbf{y})\bigr)_j \, , \quad j = 1,\dots,N \, ,
\end{equation}
where \texttt{dst} and \texttt{idst} denote the Discrete Sine Transform and its inverse, respectively. The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication, and the vector that contains the negative squared wave numbers is in the form $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2 ]^T$.

The full discrete operator $L_{\mathrm{op}}$ is then assembled by addition
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = -\Delta_h \mathbf{y} + q(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
where it holds that $\Delta_h \mathbf{y} = \mathrm{idst}(k \odot \mathrm{dst}(\mathbf{y})) = D_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$. For the Paine equation with the potential term operator $E_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$, it is
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = -D_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) + E_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and for the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential term operator $Q_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = -D_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) + Q_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) \, .
\end{equation}


The corresponding matrix representation $A$ is obtained by applying the operator $L_{\mathrm{op}}$ to the columns of the identity matrix. The eigenvalues of the resulting matrix $A$ approximate the eigenvalues of the continuous problem with spectral accuracy for sufficiently smooth potentials.

The implementation of the method for the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations is described in Algorithms \ref{alg_DST_Paine} and \ref{alg_DST_CE}, respectively.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Paine equation}
\label{alg_DST_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $x_i = \frac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^T$
\BlankLine
\State $D_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $E_{op}(y) = \exp(x) \odot y$
\State $L_{op}(y) = -D_{op}(y) + E_{op}(y)$
\State $A = L_{op}(I_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_DST_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + \frac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^T$
\BlankLine
\State $D_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $Q_{op}(y) = \big( \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x) \big) \odot y$
\State $L_{op}(y) = -D_{op}(y) + Q_{op}(y)$
\State $A = L_{op}(I_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Asymptotic behaviour}
\noindent
Next, we want to test the asymptotic equation from the paper \cite{fix} for both the Paine and Coffey–Evans equations. That means comparing our eigenvalues from above with the asymptotic curve that follows from the Theorem \ref{theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic expansion for Dirichlet boundary conditions]\label{theorem}
Let $\lambda_k$ be the k-th eigenvalue of
\begin{equation}
    u'' + [\lambda - p(x)]u = 0 \, , \quad 0 \leq x \leq \pi \, ,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
Then 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k & + (2\pi k)^{-1}P_1(\pi) \\[2pt]
    & + (4 \pi k^3)^{-1}\left[ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)P_2(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{\pi} \right) P_1(\pi)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)p'(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) p'(0)  \right] \\[2pt]
    & + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    P_\ell(\pi) = \int_0^\pi p(t)^\ell\,\mathrm{d}t \, .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Now we use this theorem to derive asymptotes for our two test problems. The asymptote for Paine equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{e^{\pi} - 1}{2 \pi k} + \frac{1}{4 \pi k^3} \left( \frac{e^{2 \pi} -1}{4} - \frac{(e^{\pi}-1)^2}{\pi} + \frac{e^{\pi}}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \right) + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and asymptote for the Coffey--Evans equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{225}{k} + \frac{38 192.75}{k^3} - \frac{50625}{k^3} + O(k^{-4}) \, .
\end{equation}

\chapter{Semi-discrete simulations of Wave propagation}
\noindent
The focus of this chapter will be on the time evolution of the wave equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave}
    \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial t^2 } = c^2 \, \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial x^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
where we choose $c = 1$.

And we will be mainly interested in the time evolution of some combination of functions in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave_general}
    u(x,t) = \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx \pm \omega_l t )} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the relation between wave number and angular frequency is $\omega_k = c  k$ and $\omega_l = c  l$.

We will use approximation techniques for the discretization of the second space derivative present in the equation \ref{eq_wave}. In every simulation, the time evolution is exact. We discretize only in space to fairly compare our discretization techniques. Two different cases of boundary conditions will be of interest to us: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item periodic boundary conditions,
    \item dirichlet boundary conditions.
\end{itemize}
And time evolution of three cases of wave \ref{eq_wave_general} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item one wave  $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)}$,
    \item two waves (same direction) $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx - \omega_l t )}$,
    \item two waves (opposite direction) $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx + \omega_l t )}$.
\end{itemize}


In our simulations we would compare in dimensionless time units. We do semi-discrete simulations because of exact time evolution and space approximations.


\section{Exact time evolution}
\noindent
To compare different spatial discretization techniques fairly, we discretize the wave equation only in space and solve the resulting system exactly in time. 

Our focus is wave equation \ref{eq_wave} with Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$. Instead of using numerical time-stepping methods (such as leapfrog), we employ exact time integration based on the eigen-decomposition of the spatial operator. The procedure is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We discretize the spatial operator and define the matrix $A$ as an approximation of the negative second derivative:
    \[
    A \approx -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}.
    \]

    \item Substituting into the wave equation \eqref{eq_wave} (with $c=1$) yields the semi-discrete system
    \[
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = -A \mathbf{u}.
    \]

    \item Next, we solve the eigenvalue problem for the matrix $A$:
    \[
    A \mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k,
    \]
    where $\lambda_k > 0$ are the eigenvalues and $\mathbf{v}_k$ are the corresponding eigenvectors. From these we obtain the angular frequencies
    \[
    \omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}.
    \]

    \item Substituting from above 
    \[ 
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} + \boldsymbol{\omega}^2 \mathbf{u} = 0
    \]
    where \[ \mathbf{u(t)} = \sum_k u_k(t)  = \sum_k c_k(t) v_k(x) \]
    \item We project the initial conditions $u(x,0)$ and $u_t(x,0)$ onto the eigenbasis to obtain the modal coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$.

    \item Each mode then evolves independently according to the equation of a harmonic oscillator:
    \[
    \frac{d^2 c_k}{dt^2} + \omega_k^2 c_k = 0.
    \]
    The analytical solution of this second-order ODE is
    \[
    c_k(t) = a_k \cos(\omega_k t) + b_k \sin(\omega_k t).
    \]

    \item Finally, we reconstruct the full solution at any time $t$ by superposition:
    \[
    u(x,t) = \sum_k c_k(t) \, v_k(x).
    \]
\end{enumerate}

This method ensures that the time evolution is \textbf{exact} — there is no time discretization error. The only approximation (and thus the only source of error) comes from the spatial discretization of the second derivative operator.



\subsection*{Methods used in the code}
\noindent
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DST (Discrete Sine Transform)}: Uses exact sine modes $\sin(kx)$ on $[0, \pi]$. The frequencies are analytically $\omega_k = c \cdot k$. This is the most accurate method for Dirichlet boundary conditions.
    
    \item \textbf{FD, Numerov, CDM, MBCD, LGCC}: These methods construct a matrix approximation of the second derivative, compute its eigenvalues and eigenvectors using \texttt{eig}, and then apply the same analytical time evolution as above.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Mathematical formulation}
\noindent
For any of the methods, the solution at time $t$ is given by:
\[
\mathbf{u}(t) = V \Big( \mathbf{a} \cdot \cos(\boldsymbol{\omega} t) + \mathbf{b} \cdot \sin(\boldsymbol{\omega} t) \Big),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V$ is the matrix of eigenvectors,
    \item $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the vector of angular frequencies,
    \item $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are coefficient vectors obtained from the initial conditions.
\end{itemize}

This technique allows for direct computation of the solution at any time $t$ without time-stepping.

In this chapter, we will be interested in simulations involving the equation that describes a one-dimensional metamaterial, specifically an infinitesimal lattice system that is built from cells with inner mass. The metamaterial is in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures_meta/pic_1.png}
    \caption{Figure of one dimensional infinite lattice metamaterial.}
    \label{fig_metamaterial}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Standard equations}
\noindent
An intuitive description based on classical newtonian motion is given by the equations \ref{eq_newton_metamaterial}.

\begin{equation}\label{eq_newton_metamaterial}
    \begin{cases}
        m_1^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{dt^2} + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j+1)}\right) + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j-1)} \right) + k_2 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_2^{(j)} \right) = 0 \\[10pt]
        m_2^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_2^{(j)}}{d t^2} +  k_2 \left( u_2^{(j)} - u_1^{(j)}  \right) = 0
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}


\subsection*{Rate-type equations}
\noindent
But we would like to work with rate-type equations. Derivation from the classical Newtonian equations \ref{eq_newton_metamaterial} to rate-type equations \ref{eq_rate_type} is done precisely in \cite{cit1}.


\begin{equation}\label{eq_rate_type}
    \begin{cases}
        m_1^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{d t^2} - \sigma^{(j)}  = 0\; \\[10pt]
        \dfrac{m_2^{(j)}}{k_2} \dfrac{d^2 \sigma^{(j)}}{d t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2^{(j)}}{m_1^{(j)}} \right) \sigma^{(j)}  = & \dfrac{m_2^{(j)} k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{d^2 }{d t^2 } \left( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \right) \\
        & + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \right) \; \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

If we wanted to see how the continuous form looks, we substitute
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2 } \approx \dfrac{u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)}}{L^2}
\end{equation}
The continuous model has a specific form.
\begin{equation}\label{eq_rate_type_continuous}
    \begin{cases}
        m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - \sigma  = 0\; \\[10pt]
        \dfrac{m_2}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \sigma}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma  = \dfrac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \right) + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \; \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Now we will rewrite the problem to more suitable form for numerical simulations \cite{tozzi}. Denote vectors with bold font and use approximation for the differentiation matrix $\mathbb{A}$
\begin{equation}\label{eq_rate_type}
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{m_1} \dfrac{d^2 \mathbf{u}}{d t^2} - \boldsymbol{\sigma} = 0\; \\[10pt]
        \dfrac{\mathbf{m_2}}{k_2} \dfrac{d^2 \boldsymbol{\sigma}}{d t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{\mathbf{m_2}}{\mathbf{m_1}} \right) \boldsymbol{\sigma}  = \dfrac{\mathbf{m_2} k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{d^2 }{d t^2 } \left( \mathbb{A} \mathbf{u} \right) + k_1 \left( \mathbb{A} \mathbf{u} \right) \; \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Rewrite as
\begin{align*}
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{d^2 \mathbf{u}}{dt^2}=\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m _1}}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\\[10pt]
        \dfrac{d^2 }{dt^2}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\sigma}- k_1\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}\Bigr)=-\dfrac{k_2}{\mathbf{m_2}}\Biggl(1+\dfrac{\mathbf{m_2}}{\mathbf{m_1}}\Biggr)\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\dfrac{k_2 k_1}{\mathbf{m_2}}\Bigl(\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}\Bigr).
    \end{cases}
\end{align*}
Defining 
\begin{gather*}
    \mathbf{a}:= \boldsymbol{\sigma}- k_1\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u},
\end{gather*}
we get the following system of ordinary differential equations for the unknowns $\mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{a}$:
\begin{align}\label{}
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{d^2 }{dt^2}\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{k_1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}+\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m _1}}\mathbf{a},\\[10pt]
        \dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathbf{a}=-\dfrac{k_2k_1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}-k_2\Biggl(\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m_2}}+\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\Biggr)\mathbf{a}.
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
It can be usefull to transforme it into a first order system, introducing the auxiliary functions 
$$\mathbf{v}:=\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{u},\quad \mathbf{b}:=\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{a}$$
\begin{align}\label{eq_system_meta}
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{u}= \mathbf{v},\\[5pt]
        \dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{a}= \mathbf{b},\\[5pt]
        \dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{v}=\dfrac{k_1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}+\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\mathbf{a},\\[5pt]
        \dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{b}=-\dfrac{k_2k_1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\mathbb{A}\mathbf{u}-k_2\Biggl(\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m_2}}+\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{m_1}}\Biggr)\mathbf{a}.
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
So we will perform simulations of time evolution using these equations \ref{eq_system_meta}, where we will change the matrix $\mathbb{A}$ to the differentiation matrix, using FD, Numerov, DFT, CDM, MBCDM, and LGCC.

The difference from previous chapter is that now we dont have an exact solution, so we will compare all these methods with DFT (DST), because this was the most precise of them all.

\subsection{Dispersion relation - V PROCESU}
\noindent
So, lets now look in more detail at why for higher wave numbers $k$ the slowing effect is greater for FD approximation. On a uniform grid, we have $x_j = j \; \Delta x$, $t_n = n \; \Delta t$ so that means we can approximate
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} \right|_{j,n} \approx \frac{u_{j}^{n+1} - 2 \; u_j^n + u_j^{n-1}}{(\Delta t)^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \right|_{j,n} \approx \frac{u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \; u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n}}{(\Delta x)^2} \, .
\end{equation}
Substituting into the wave equation \ref{eq_wave}, we get the leapfrog discrete scheme
\begin{equation}
    u_{j}^{n+1} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_j^{n-1} = c^2 \, \frac{(\Delta t)^2}{(\Delta x)^2} \, \left( u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n} \right) \, ,
\end{equation}
which we can rewrite as
\begin{equation}
    u_{j}^{n+1} = 2 \, u_j^n - u_j^{n-1} + r^2 \, \left( u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n} \right) \, .
\end{equation}
Next, choose a solution in the form
\begin{equation}
    u_j^n = e^{i \, (k \, j \, \Delta x \, - \, \omega \, n \, \Delta t)} \, ,
\end{equation}
which, after substitution, gives
\begin{equation}
    e^{-i \omega \Delta t} - 2 + e^{i \omega \Delta t} = r^2 (e^{-i k \Delta x} - 2 + e^{i k \Delta x}) \, .
\end{equation}
Using Euler's formula
\begin{equation}
    -4 \sin^2{\frac{\omega \Delta t}{2}} = r^2 (-4\sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}})
\end{equation}
$r>0, \omega >0$
\begin{equation}
    \sin{\frac{\omega \Delta t}{2}} = r \left| \sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}} \right|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\omega = \frac{2}{\Delta t} \arcsin{(r \sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})})}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\omega = \frac{2}{\Delta t} \arcsin{(c\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})})} \approx \frac{2c}{\Delta x} |\sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})}|
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \omega_{num}(k) = \frac{2c}{\Delta x} \left| \sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}} \right|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{exact}(k) = c \left| k \right| 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    |\sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}}| \leq |\frac{k \Delta x}{2}|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{num}(k) \leq c |k| = \omega_{exact}(k)
\end{equation}
The equation holds only for $k \rightarrow 0$. Thus, waves approximated by FD propagate slower. The graphical form of this result can be seen in the Figure \ref{fig_dispersion}.
