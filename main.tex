\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\noindent
In this work, we will first focus on the eigenvalue computations of the Sturm-Liouville and Coffey-Evans equations using several different methods for the discretization of the operator of the second derivative. We will compute our data and compare it to one another.

Next, we will study the classical wave equation and test finite differences and fast fourier transform on their propagation.

Finally, we will focus on rate-type models for metamaterials. Our goal will be to use them for simulations of wave propagation.

Metamaterials are, by definition, “A synthetic composite material engineered to display properties not usually found in natural materials.”

These exotic properties are usually related to wave propagation phenomena in metamaterials, and they typically stem from a carefully designed microstructure.  

The thesis will focus on a particularly promising class of models (rate-type models) for metamaterials behavior. Both qualitative (refinement of models, qualitative features) and quantitative (numerical simulations) analyzes are needed.
	

\chapter{Theoretical framework}
\noindent
In this chapter, we will describe the core notions that will be used in the rest of this thesis. Concepts such as metamaterials, spectral methods, and rate-type models.

\section{Metamaterials}
\noindent
Metamaterials are artificially engineered composite structures designed to exhibit extraordinary physical properties not typically found in natural materials.

Examples of such properties include a negative refractive index, negative effective mass density, or negative bulk modulus, which enable phenomena like wave cloaking, superlensing, and band gap formation for controlling electromagnetic, acoustic, and elastic waves.

These properties arise from the precise arrangement of subwavelength unit cells or microstructures, rather than the intrinsic characteristics of the constituent materials, allowing for tailored responses to external stimuli through geometric design and material selection.



\section{Spectral methods}
\noindent
Spectral methods represent a class of numerical techniques for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) and other mathematical problems, characterized by their use of global basis functions to approximate solutions. Spectral methods expand the solution as a series of orthogonal polynomials or trigonometric functions, such as Fourier series for periodic problems or Chebyshev polynomials for non-periodic domains.

This global approximation leads to high-order accuracy, often achieving exponential convergence for smooth functions, as the error decreases faster than any algebraic power of the number of basis functions.

The core idea is to transform the problem into the spectral space, where differentiation becomes a simple algebraic operation via differentiation matrices, and then solve the resulting system efficiently \cite{cit2}.

One of the key advantages of spectral methods lies in their efficiency for problems with smooth solutions, where they require fewer degrees of freedom compared to low-order methods to achieve comparable accuracy.

For instance, in the context of wave propagation or elastodynamics, spectral methods preserve essential wave properties, such as phase and group velocities, without introducing artificial dissipation or dispersion that plagues finite difference schemes. 

In applications to metamaterials and elastic wave modeling, spectral methods excel in capturing dispersive behaviors and band gaps inherent to these engineered structures. By leveraging Fourier transforms for periodic lattices or Chebyshev expansions for bounded domains, they accurately resolve high-frequency modes without the numerical artifacts that could distort physical interpretations. As illustrated in \cite{cit2}, examples like the one-dimensional wave equation solved via Fourier spectral methods highlight their ability to maintain energy conservation and spectral accuracy over long simulation times.

This makes spectral methods an ideal choice for theoretical and computational studies in applied mathematics and materials science, providing a robust framework for validating continuum models against discrete lattice systems.



\section{Rate-type models}
\noindent
Rate-type constitutive models, also known as rate-dependent constitutive relations, represent a class of material descriptions in continuum mechanics where the stress (or force) is expressed as a function of the rate of deformation, its time derivatives, and possibly the stress itself, rather than solely depending on the current deformation state as in classical hyperelasticity.

Originating from the works of Truesdell (1955) and Oldroyd (1950), these models incorporate objective time derivatives of the stress tensor to ensure frame-indifference, making them particularly suitable for capturing dynamic and viscoelastic behaviors in complex materials without violating fundamental physical principles like positive definite mass density.

In the context of metamaterials, as demonstrated in \cite{cit1}, rate-type relations avoid the unphysical notion of negative effective mass by embedding the effects of internal microstructures—such as local resonances in mass-in-mass systems—into the constitutive equation for stress $\sigma$, which includes second-order time derivatives of $\sigma$ coupled with spatial derivatives of displacement. This approach maintains Newton's second law in its standard form while accurately reproducing dispersive wave propagation and band gaps, providing a physically consistent framework for modeling elastic waves in engineered structures like those considered in this thesis.


\chapter{Eigenvalue computations}
\noindent
The aim of this chapter is, first, to introduce two test problems: namely, the Sturm-Liouville and the Coffey-Evans equations. Then, we will describe methods that discretize these problems and allow us to solve them approximately. Mainly, we will focus on how to discretize the operator of the second derivative.

The methods that will be described and used are: finite differences, Chebyshev differentiation, Mapped barycentric Chebyshev differentiation, Discrete sine transform, Numerov, and the Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev collocation. 

Our goal is to compare all of these methods. We will do this by applying the discretizations and computing eigenvalues. For our comparison, we need some reference values. These will be computed using the Matslise program.

So, we will judge how close each discretization was to the reference values computed by Matslise.

\section{Test problems}
\noindent
In this section, we will introduce two test problems on which we will apply the discretizations. The general form of these problems is
\begin{equation}\label{eq_general_test_problem}
    -u''(x) + q(x) \, u(x) = \lambda \, u(x) \, , \quad x \in (a, \, b) \, , \quad u(a) = u(b) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}



\subsection{Sturm-Liouville equation}
\noindent
The first of the two is the Sturm-Liouville (SL) equation, in one special case where
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        q(x) & = e^x \, , \\[4pt]
        a & = 0 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi \ . \\[2pt]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
So, by substituting these values to \ref{eq_general_test_problem} the resulting equation has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_SL}
    -u''(x) + e^x \, u(x) = \lambda \, u(x) \, , \quad x \in (0, \, \pi) \, , \quad u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}



\subsection{Coffey-Evans equation}
\noindent 
The second one is the Coffey-Evans (CE) equation with
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE_coeffs}
    \begin{split}
        q(x) & = \beta^2 \, \sin^2{(2 \, x)} -2 \, \beta \, \cos{(2 \, x)} \, , \\[4pt]
        a & = -\pi/2 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi/2 \, . \\[2pt]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute \ref{eq_CE_coeffs} in \ref{eq_general_test_problem} we get
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE}
    \begin{split}
    -u''(x) + \left[\beta^2 \, \sin^2{(2 \, x)} -2 \, \beta \, \cos{(2 \, x)} \right] \, u(x) & = \lambda \, u(x) \, , \\
     u(-\pi/2) = u(\pi/2) & = 0 \, , \\
    -\pi/2 < x & < \pi/2  \, .\\
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
In the whole text, we will use the value of $\beta = 30$.



\section{Discretization methods}
\noindent
In this section, discretization methods that will be used for computations of the eigenvalues of the abovementioned test problems will be described.

Our aim is to discretize the operator of the second derivative by using an approximation matrix
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \, x^2} \approx D \, .
\end{equation}
And in all cases, use MATLAB's \texttt{eig} function for the computation of eigenvalues.

In both cases, our goal will be to rewrite the main equation \ref{eq_general_test_problem} to the form
\begin{equation}
    \left( -D + A  \right) \, u(x) = \lambda \, u(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
where for the SL equation, we will take 
\begin{equation}
    A = E = \texttt{diag}(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}) \, ,
\end{equation}
so we get
\begin{equation}
    \left( -D + E  \right) \, u(x) = \lambda \, u(x) \, .
\end{equation}
And in the case of CE equation, the situation will be analogous 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    A = Q = \texttt{diag}(-2 \, \beta \, \cos{(2 \, x_1)} + \beta^2 \, \sin^2{(2 \, x_1)} \, , \dots, \\ -2 \, \beta \, \cos{(2 \, x_N)} + \beta^2 \, \sin^2{(2 \, x_N)} ) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
so we get
\begin{equation}
    \left( -D + Q  \right) \, u(x) = \lambda \, u(x) \, .
\end{equation} 



\subsection{Matslise application}
\noindent
To obtain highly accurate reference eigenvalues of our test problems, a MATLAB program named Matslise has been implemented. Matslise is based on shooting and piecewise perturbation methods. In this thesis, it serves as the primary reference solver for computing benchmark eigenvalues and for validating results obtained by other numerical methods \cite{matslise}.



\subsection{Finite difference method}
\noindent
The first numerical method on our list is the Finite Difference method (FD). The case of this method, which we will be using, approximates the second derivative on a discrete grid using the second-order central finite difference formula
\begin{equation}
    u''(x_i) \approx \frac{u(x_i - h) - 2 \, u(x_i) + u(x_i + h) }{h^2} \; .
\end{equation}
It's matrix form is
\begin{equation}
D = \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{bmatrix}
    2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
    0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & \cdots & \vdots \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 2 \\ 
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
and the implementation of the finite difference method is in algorithms \ref{alg_1} and \ref{alg_2}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite difference method - SL}
\label{alg_1}  
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i \cdot h$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\BlankLine
\State Create tridiagonal matrix $D}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $D_{i,i} = 2 / h^2$ \quad \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State \quad $D_{i,i+1} = D_{i+1,i} = -1 / h^2$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $E = \texttt{diag}(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N})$
\State $A = -D + E$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$ 
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite difference method - CE}
\label{alg_2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $h = \pi/(N+1)$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + i \cdot h$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\BlankLine
\State Create tridiagonal matrix $D$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $D_{i,i}   = 2/h^2$  \quad \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State \quad $D_{i,i+1} = D_{i+1,i} = -1/h^2$  \quad for $i=1$ to $N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $q_i = \beta^2 \, \sin^2(2 \, x_i) - 2 \, \beta \, \cos(2 \, x_i)$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State $Q = \texttt{diag}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N)$
\State $A = -D + Q$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Numerov method}
\noindent
Numerov's method \cite{numerov} uses higher-order finite differences and approximates the eigenvalues of the equation \ref{eq_general_test_problem} with an equation in the form
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    b \, u(x_{i-1}) + a \, u(x_i ) + c \, u(x_{i+1}) = h^2 \, \lambda \, \left[u(x_{i-1}) + 10 \, u(x_i) + u(x_{i+1}) \right] \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
with coefficients
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        a & = + 24 + 10 \, h^2 \, q(x_i) \, ,   \\
        b & = -12 + h^2 \, q(x_{i-1}) \, ,  \\
    c & = -12 + h^2 \, q(x_{i+1}) \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
This approximation can be rewritten in matrix form
\begin{equation}
    A \, u(x) = \lambda \, B \, u(x)
\end{equation}
where matrices A and B are
\begin{equation}
A = \begin{bmatrix}
    a & c & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    b & a & c & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & b & a & c & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & b & a & \cdots & \vdots \\
    \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & a  \\
\end{bmatrix} ,
\label{}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B = h^2\begin{bmatrix}
    10 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & 10 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 10 & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 10  \\
\end{bmatrix} .
\label{}
\end{equation}




\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Numerov method - SL}
\label{alg_3}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i \cdot h$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\State $q_i = \exp(x_i)$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\BlankLine
\State Create tridiagonal matrix $A$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $A_{i,i} = 24 + 10 h^2 q_i$ \quad \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State \quad $A_{i,i+1} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N-1$
\State \quad $A_{i+1,i} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N-1$
\BlankLine
\State Create tridiagonal matrix $B$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $B_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State \quad $B_{i,i+1} = h^2$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N-1$
\State \quad $B_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A, B) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1$ to $N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12  s_k^2  /  (h^2  (3 - s_k^2))$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}} \leq \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}} \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Chebyshev differentiation matrix method}
\noindent
The Chebyshev Differentiation Datrix method (CDM) is a high-order spectral collocation technique that approximates the derivatives of a function at specific collocation points using global polynomial interpolation \cite{cdm}, \cite{trefethen}. It will be implemented using the MATLAB chebfun package.

In this approach, we first generate the Chebyshev points of the second kind via \texttt{chebpts} on the interval $[-1,1]$ using the Chebfun package. 

Then, the full differentiation matrix $D_{\text{full}}$ of the second order is computed using the \texttt{diffmat} function.

For problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(0)=u(\pi)=0$ or $u(-\pi/2)=u(\pi/2)=0$, the boundary rows and columns are removed to obtain the reduced interior differentiation matrix $D_{\text{Cheb}}$.

After mapping the interior Chebyshev nodes from $[-1, \, 1]$ to the desired physical interval ($[0, \, \pi]$ or $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$) with an appropriate scaling factor, the discrete second-derivative operator is formed.

Finally, the differential equation is converted into a matrix eigenvalue problem of the form $A\mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbf{u}$, where the matrix $A$ combines the scaled differentiation matrix with the potential term. The eigenvalues are obtained by solving this eigenvalue problem.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev differentiation matrix - SL}
\label{alg_5}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{Cheb}} = D_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \; 2:N+1]$
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \frac{\pi}{2} \, (t_{\text{int},i} + 1)$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N$
\State $D = (2 / \pi)^2 \cdot D_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $E = \texttt{diag}(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N})$
\State $A = -D + E$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev differentiation matrix – CE}
\label{alg_6}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $D_{Cheb} = D_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, 2:N+1]$ 
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot t_{\text{int},i}$ \quad for $i=1$ to $N$

\State $Q = \texttt{diag}(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N)$
\State $D = (2 / \pi)^2 \cdot D_{Cheb}$
\State $A = -D + Q$
\BlankLine

\subsection{Mapped barycentric Chebyshev differentiation matrix method}
\noindent
The Mapped barycentric Chebyshev differentiation matrix (MBCDM) method is a high-order spectral collocation technique designed for solving Sturm-Liouville and Coffey-Evans eigenvalue equations on a finite interval with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. 

It employs a Kosloff-Tal-Ezer (KTE) mapping with parameter $\alpha$ to redistribute the Chebyshev nodes, providing better resolution near the boundaries and improved numerical stability for problems with steep gradients or highly oscillatory solutions.

The second derivative is constructed in the computational domain using barycentric differentiation matrices and then transformed to the physical domain $[0,\, \pi]$ or $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$ via the chain rule. After incorporating the potential term, the discrete operator is reduced by imposing the boundary conditions, resulting in a standard matrix eigenvalue problem whose eigenvalues approximate the continuous spectrum with spectral accuracy \cite{cdm}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped barycentric Chebyshev differentiation - SL}
\label{}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\alpha$
\State $s =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $\text{asin}_\alpha = \arcsin(\alpha)$
\State $g(s) = \arcsin(\alpha \cdot s) / \text{asin}_\alpha$ 
\State $x = (g(s) + 1) \cdot \pi/2$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $g'(s) = \dfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-(\alpha s)^2} \cdot \text{asin}_\alpha}$
\State \quad $g''(s) = \dfrac{\alpha^3 s}{(1-\alpha^2 s^2)^{3/2} \cdot \text{asin}_\alpha}$
\BlankLine
\State $dx/ds = \pi/2 \cdot g'(s)$
\State $d^2x/ds^2 = \pi/2 \cdot g''(s)$
\State $D_s =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, \, 1)
\State $D_s^{2} = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, \, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $D_2 = \texttt{diag}\left[1/(dx/ds)^2\right] \cdot D_s^{2} - \texttt{diag}\left[(d^2x/ds^2)/(dx/ds)^3\right] \cdot D_s$
\State $E = \texttt{diag}(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_{N+2}})$
\State $A = -D_2 + E$
\State $A_{\text{int}} = A(2:N+1, 2:N+1)$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($A_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\caption{Mapped barycentric Chebyshev differentiation - CE}
\label{}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\subsection{Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev collocation method}
\noindent
The Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev collocation (LGCC) method combines spectral Galerkin projection with Chebyshev collocation to solve eigenvalue problems of the form \ref{eq_general_test_problem}.

The method uses the change of variables $t = \frac{2x - (b+a)}{b-a} \in [-1,1]$ to map the interval to the standard domain. The test functions are chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(t) = L_k(t) - L_{k+2}(t), \quad k = 0,1,\dots,m-1,
\end{equation}
where $L_k(t)$ denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree $k$, computed with the chebfun \texttt{legpoly} function. These basis functions automatically satisfy the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The weak form of the problem is projected onto this basis, leading to the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
    (S + \hat{M}) \, \mathbf{v} = \mu \, T \, \mathbf{v},
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the (diagonal) stiffness matrix, $T$ is the mass matrix (penta-diagonal), and $\hat{M}$ is the symmetrized potential matrix obtained via Chebyshev interpolation of $q(t) \, \phi_j(t)$ followed by extraction of Legendre coefficients.

The eigenvalues $\mu_k$ are then rescaled to the original problem $\lambda_k = \frac{4}{\pi^2} \mu_k$ (for the standard interval length $\pi$). The method achieves high accuracy for smooth potentials thanks to the spectral convergence of Legendre and Chebyshev expansions, while the basis choice ensures exact enforcement of boundary conditions \cite{legendre_galerkin}. 

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre-Galerkin-Chebyshev collocation - SL}
\label{}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Stiffness matrix $S$ (diagonal):
\State \quad $S_{k, \, k} = 4 \, k + 6$ \quad for $k = 0$ to $m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Mass matrix $T$ (penta-diagonal):
\State \quad $T_{k,k} = \frac{2}{2 \, k+1} + \frac{2}{2 \, k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0$ to $m-1$
\State \quad $T_{k, \, k+2} = T_{k+2, \, k} = -\frac{2}{2 \,k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0$ to $m-3$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $q(t) = \frac{\pi^2}{4} \exp\left( \frac{\pi}{2} (t + 1) \right)$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $\phi_k(t) = L_{k}(t) - L_{k+2}(t)$ \quad for $k = 0$ to $m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Matrix $M$ (Galerkin projection of potential):
\State \quad for $j = 0$ to $m-1$:
\State \quad \quad $f_j(t) = q(t) \cdot \phi_j(t)$
\State \quad \quad $I_{f_j} =$ \texttt{chebfun}($f_j$,$N+1$) 
\State \quad \quad \texttt{legcoeffs}($I_{f_j}$) $ \to c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$
\State \quad \quad for $i = 0$ to $m-1$ ($k=i$):
\State \quad \quad \quad $M_{i,j} = c_{k+1} \cdot \frac{2}{2 \, k+1} - c_{k+3} \cdot \frac{2}{2 \, k+5}$ \quad (if $k+3 \leq N$)
\BlankLine
\State $\hat{M} = \texttt{triu}(M) + \texttt{triu}(M, \, 1)^T$
\State $A = S + \hat{M}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A,T) $ \to \mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_m $
\State$\lambda_k = 4 \mu_k / \pi^2$ \quad for $k = 1$ to $m$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Discrete Sine Transform}
\noindent
The Discrete Sine Transform (DST) provides a natural basis for solving problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a finite interval. 
 
The second derivative operator becomes diagonal in the DST domain. Specifically, the discrete Laplacian applied to a vector $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_N)^T$ of interior grid values is realized by
\[
(-\Delta_h \, \mathbf{y})_j = \operatorname{idst}\bigl(k_j^2 \cdot \operatorname{dst}(\mathbf{y})\bigr)_j \, , \quad j=1,\dots,N,
\]
where $\texttt{dst}$ and $\texttt{idst}$ denote the discrete sine transform and its inverse, respectively, and $k_j$ are the associated wave numbers. 

The full discrete operator $-\Delta_h + q(x)$ is then assembled by transforming to the spectral domain, performing element-wise multiplication by the potential (its discretization), and transforming back. This approach yields a matrix-free implementation (or an explicitly formed matrix when desired) whose eigenvalues approximate the continuous spectrum with spectral accuracy for sufficiently smooth potentials.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - SL}
\label{}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $q(x)$
\State $x_i = \frac{\pi}{N+1} \cdot i$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\State $k = [-(1)^2, -(2)^2, \dots, -(N)^2]^T$
\State $D_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $Q_{op}(y) = q(x) \odot y$
\State $L_{op}(y) = -D_{op}(y) + Q_{op}(y)$
\State $A = L_{op}(I_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - CE}
\label{}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $q(x)$, $\beta$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + \frac{\pi}{N+1} \cdot i$ \quad for $i = 1$ to $N$
\State $k = [-(1)^2, -(2)^2, \dots, -(N)^2]^T$
\State $D_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $Q_{op}(y) = q(x) \odot y$
\State $L_{op}(y) = -D_{op}(y) + Q_{op}(y)$
\State $A = L_{op}(I_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}(A) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\chapter{Wave propagation}
\noindent
The focus of this chapter will be on the time evolution of the wave equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave}
    \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial t^2 } = c^2 \, \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial x^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
where we choose $c = 1$.

And we will be mainly interested in the time evolution of some combination of functions in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave_general}
    u(x,t) = \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx \pm \omega_l t )} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the relation between wave number and angular frequency is $\omega_k = c \cdot k$ and $\omega_l = c \cdot l$.

We will use approximation techniques for the discretization of the second space derivative present in the equation \ref{eq_wave}. In every simulation, the time evolution is exact. We discretize only in space to fairly compare our discretization techniques. Two different cases of boundary conditions will be of interest to us: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item periodic boundary conditions,
    \item dirichlet boundary conditions.
\end{itemize}
And time evolution of three cases of wave \ref{eq_wave_general} 
\begin{itemize}
    \item one wave  $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)}$,
    \item two waves (same direction) $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx - \omega_l t )}$,
    \item two waves (opposite direction) $\to \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx + \omega_l t )}$.
\end{itemize}



\section{Exact Time Evolution}
\noindent
To compare different spatial discretization techniques fairly, we discretize the wave equation only in space and solve the resulting system exactly in time. 

Our focus is wave equation \ref{eq_wave} with Dirichlet boundary conditions $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$. Instead of using numerical time-stepping methods (such as leapfrog), we employ exact time integration based on the eigen-decomposition of the spatial operator. The procedure is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We discretize the spatial operator and define the matrix $A$ as an approximation of the negative second derivative:
    \[
    A \approx -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}.
    \]

    \item Substituting into the wave equation \eqref{eq_wave} (with $c=1$) yields the semi-discrete system
    \[
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = -A \mathbf{u}.
    \]

    \item Next, we solve the eigenvalue problem for the matrix $A$:
    \[
    A \mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k,
    \]
    where $\lambda_k > 0$ are the eigenvalues and $\mathbf{v}_k$ are the corresponding eigenvectors. From these we obtain the angular frequencies
    \[
    \omega_k = \sqrt{\lambda_k}.
    \]

    \item Substituting from above 
    \[ 
    \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} + \boldsymbol{\omega}^2 \mathbf{u} = 0
    \]
    where \[ \mathbf{u(t)} = \sum_k u_k(t)  = \sum_k c_k(t) v_k(x) \]
    \item We project the initial conditions $u(x,0)$ and $u_t(x,0)$ onto the eigenbasis to obtain the modal coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$.

    \item Each mode then evolves independently according to the equation of a harmonic oscillator:
    \[
    \frac{d^2 c_k}{dt^2} + \omega_k^2 c_k = 0.
    \]
    The analytical solution of this second-order ODE is
    \[
    c_k(t) = a_k \cos(\omega_k t) + b_k \sin(\omega_k t).
    \]

    \item Finally, we reconstruct the full solution at any time $t$ by superposition:
    \[
    u(x,t) = \sum_k c_k(t) \, v_k(x).
    \]
\end{enumerate}

This method ensures that the time evolution is \textbf{exact} — there is no time discretization error. The only approximation (and thus the only source of error) comes from the spatial discretization of the second derivative operator.



\subsection*{Methods used in the code}
\noindent
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DST (Discrete Sine Transform)}: Uses exact sine modes $\sin(kx)$ on $[0, \pi]$. The frequencies are analytically $\omega_k = c \cdot k$. This is the most accurate method for Dirichlet boundary conditions.
    
    \item \textbf{FD, Numerov, CDM, MBCD, LGCC}: These methods construct a matrix approximation of the second derivative, compute its eigenvalues and eigenvectors using \texttt{eig}, and then apply the same analytical time evolution as above.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Mathematical formulation}
\noindent
For any of the methods, the solution at time $t$ is given by:
\[
\mathbf{u}(t) = V \Big( \mathbf{a} \cdot \cos(\boldsymbol{\omega} t) + \mathbf{b} \cdot \sin(\boldsymbol{\omega} t) \Big),
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V$ is the matrix of eigenvectors,
    \item $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the vector of angular frequencies,
    \item $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are coefficient vectors obtained from the initial conditions.
\end{itemize}

This technique allows for direct computation of the solution at any time $t$ without time-stepping.

\subsubsection*{Rate-type equations}
\noindent
But we would like to work with rate-type equations. Derivation from the classical Newtonian equations \ref{eq_newton_metamaterial} to rate-type equations \ref{eq_rate_type} is done precisely in \cite{cit1}.


\begin{equation}\label{eq_rate_type}
    \begin{cases}
        m_1^{(j)} \frac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{d t^2} - \sigma^{(j)}  = 0\; \\[10pt]
        \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{k_2} \frac{d^2 \sigma^{(j)}}{d t^2}  + \left(1 + \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{m_1^{(j)}} \right) \sigma^{(j)}  = & \frac{m_2^{(j)} k_1}{k_2} \frac{d^2 }{d t^2 } \left( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \right) \\
        & + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \right) \; \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Substituting
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2 } \approx \frac{u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)}}{L^2}
\end{equation}
The continuous model has a specific form.
\begin{equation}\label{eq_rate_type_continuous}

        \frac{m_2}{k_2} \frac{\partial^2 \sigma}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \frac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma  = \frac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \frac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \right) + k_1 L^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \; \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dispersion relation - V PROCESU}
\noindent
So, lets now look in more detail at why for higher wave numbers $k$ the slowing effect is greater for FD approximation. On a uniform grid, we have $x_j = j \; \Delta x$, $t_n = n \; \Delta t$ so that means we can approximate
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} \right|_{j,n} \approx \frac{u_{j}^{n+1} - 2 \; u_j^n + u_j^{n-1}}{(\Delta t)^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \left. \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \right|_{j,n} \approx \frac{u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \; u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n}}{(\Delta x)^2} \, .
\end{equation}
Substituting into the wave equation \ref{eq_wave}, we get the leapfrog discrete scheme
\begin{equation}
    u_{j}^{n+1} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_j^{n-1} = c^2 \, \frac{(\Delta t)^2}{(\Delta x)^2} \, \left( u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n} \right) \, ,
\end{equation}
which we can rewrite as
\begin{equation}
    u_{j}^{n+1} = 2 \, u_j^n - u_j^{n-1} + r^2 \, \left( u_{j+1}^{n} - 2 \, u_j^n + u_{j-1}^{n} \right) \, .
\end{equation}
Next, choose a solution in the form
\begin{equation}
    u_j^n = e^{i \, (k \, j \, \Delta x \, - \, \omega \, n \, \Delta t)} \, ,
\end{equation}
which, after substitution, gives
\begin{equation}
    e^{-i \omega \Delta t} - 2 + e^{i \omega \Delta t} = r^2 (e^{-i k \Delta x} - 2 + e^{i k \Delta x}) \, .
\end{equation}
Using Euler's formula
\begin{equation}
    -4 \sin^2{\frac{\omega \Delta t}{2}} = r^2 (-4\sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}})
\end{equation}
$r>0, \omega >0$
\begin{equation}
    \sin{\frac{\omega \Delta t}{2}} = r \left| \sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}} \right|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\omega = \frac{2}{\Delta t} \arcsin{(r \sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})})}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\omega = \frac{2}{\Delta t} \arcsin{(c\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x} \sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})})} \approx \frac{2c}{\Delta x} |\sin{(\frac{k \Delta x}{2})}|
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \omega_{num}(k) = \frac{2c}{\Delta x} \left| \sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}} \right|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{exact}(k) = c \left| k \right| 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    |\sin{\frac{k \Delta x}{2}}| \leq |\frac{k \Delta x}{2}|
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \omega_{num}(k) \leq c |k| = \omega_{exact}(k)
\end{equation}
The equation holds only for $k \rightarrow 0$. Thus, waves approximated by FD propagate slower. The graphical form of this result can be seen in the figure \ref{fig_dispersion}.

    \caption{Comparison of exact dispersion relation and numerical dispersion relation for wave equation.}
