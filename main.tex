\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\noindent
The accurate numerical approximation of the second derivative operator plays a central role in the solution of many important problems in applied mathematics and physics. This includes Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problems, as well as the simulation of wave propagation phenomena in various physical systems. 

When solving such problems on a computer, the continuous differential operator must be replaced by a discrete matrix that approximates its action on a finite grid. The quality of this spatial discretization is particularly important because different approaches exhibit vastly different accuracies, especially depending on the number of grid points used. This thesis focuses on a systematic comparison of several discretization methods for approximating the second derivative operator.

For problems with periodic boundary conditions, we consider the classical Finite Difference method (FD), Numerov's method (Num), and the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For problems subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the comparison is extended to include the Finite Difference method, Numerov's method, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST), the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (CDM), the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM), and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method (LGCC).

The main objective of this work is to evaluate the performance of these methods through carefully designed numerical experiments. First, we solve two regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems — the Paine equation and the Coffey--Evans equation (Coffey--Evans is the standard benchmark problem for high-index eigenvalue computation). Next, we simulate wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, considering both single waves and their superpositions. Finally, we perform analogous wave propagation simulations using the rate-type constitutive model describing a one-dimensional mass-in-mass metamaterial lattice.

A particular emphasis is placed on investigating the limiting behaviour of the metamaterial model. By gradually decreasing the internal mass parameter towards zero, we aim to numerically demonstrate the convergence of the rate-type system to the classical wave equation, as suggested by the theoretical analysis.

Throughout this thesis, we aim not only to identify the most suitable discretization method for each type of boundary condition, but also to highlight the connection between Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems and wave propagation via the dispersion relation. In particular, we show why the Discrete Sine Transform is exceptionally well-suited for Dirichlet problems: its eigenvalues are asymptotically consistent with the analytic expansion of the continuous Sturm--Liouville operator, which in turn ensures an accurate discrete dispersion relation even on relatively coarse grids.

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 1 introduces the two regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue test problems and describes our discretization techniques. \hbox{Chapter 2} presents semi-discrete simulations of wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation. Chapter 3 is devoted to semi-discrete simulations using rate-type constitutive equations for metamaterials. Finally, Chapter 4 provides a direct comparison of wave propagation in both the classical wave equation and the rate-type metamaterial~model.

\chapter{Regular Sturm--Liouville Eigenvalue Test Problems}
\noindent
The aim of this chapter is to introduce two regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue test problems: the Paine equation, first introduced in \cite{paine}, and the Coffey--Evans equation, first presented in \cite{pryce}.

Secondly, we will describe methods that discretize these continuous problems and enable us to solve them approximately (numerically). Our main focus will be on how to discretize the second derivative operator, which involves assembling the differentiation matrix and substituting it for the continuous derivative. We will implement these methods and compute the eigenvalues of the discretized problem using MATLAB \texttt{eig} function.

The discretization methods that will be introduced are: Finite Differences, Numerov's method, the Discrete Fourier Transform, the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method, and the Discrete Sine Transform. 

Our goal is to compare these discretization methods and determine the most effective one with regard to high-index eigenvalue computation. Since there is no analytical expression for the eigenvalues of the Paine/Coffey--Evans problem, we will use the eigenvalues computed by the specialized Matslise software as the reference values.

\section{Test Problems}
\noindent
In this section, we briefly introduce regular Sturm--Liouville test problems in the Schrödinger form. Their general formulation with potential $q(x)$ is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_general_test_problem}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + q(x) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \label{eq_general_SLP_1} \\[5pt]
    & u(a) = u(b) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in (a, \, b) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Next, we will define two specific cases.

\subsection{Paine Equation}
\noindent
The Paine equation has the potential
\begin{equation}
    q(x) = e^x \, ,
\end{equation}
and is defined on the interval where
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        a & = 0 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi \ .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute this into the relation \eqref{eq_general_test_problem}, we get the resulting formulation of the Paine test problem
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_SL}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + e^x u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \\[5pt]
    & u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in (0,  \, \pi) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}


\subsection{Coffey--Evans Equation}
\noindent 
The Coffey--Evans equation has the potential
\begin{equation}
    q(x) = \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} - 2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \, ,
\end{equation}
and is defined on the interval where
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE_coeffs}
    \begin{split}
        a & = - \dfrac{\pi}{2} \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \dfrac{\pi}{2} \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute this into \eqref{eq_general_test_problem}, we get the resulting formulation of the Coffey--Evans test problem
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_CE}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + \big(\beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} - 2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \big) u(x) = \lambda  u(x) \, , \\[5pt]
    & u\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = u\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in \left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2} ,  \, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Throughout the thesis, we will use the value of the parameter $\beta = 30$.


\section{Matslise Software}
\noindent
As mentioned previously, we use the Matslise software \cite{matslise} to compute reference eigenvalues. In this thesis, the reference eigenvalues are used to validate the results obtained by other numerical methods.

Matslise is a MATLAB package for the numerical solution of regular and singular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems. It belongs to the class of shooting methods: it transforms the two-point boundary value problem into an initial-value problem by guessing a trial eigenvalue $\lambda$, integrating the differential equation from one boundary to the other, and iteratively adjusting $\lambda$ until the second boundary condition is satisfied. The key innovation of Matslise is that the integration step is performed using piecewise constant perturbation methods. On each small subinterval, the potential $q(x)$ is approximated by a constant value (for which the exact analytic solution is known), and higher-order perturbation correction terms are systematically added \cite{ledoux}.

This approach is accurate even for large (high-index) eigenvalues, in which we are particularly interested, with relatively few grid points. It all makes Matslise particularly efficient and robust for our test problems.



\section{Discretization Methods}
\noindent
This section introduces the discretization methods used to approximate the second derivative operator in our test problems. These same methods will later be used for spatial discretization in wave propagation simulations.

Our aim is to discretize the continuous operator of the second derivative by using an approximation matrix
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{D} \approx \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial  x^2} \, ,
\end{equation}
which will be assembled using the methods below.

Next, we want to rewrite the main equation \eqref{eq_general_SLP_1} in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_Paine}
    \big( - \mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}  \big) \boldsymbol{u} = \lambda \boldsymbol{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
for the Paine equation, where
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}\big) \, ,
\end{equation}
and $\boldsymbol{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)\big]^\top$ is the vector of unknowns on the corresponding grid of the implemented method.

For the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(-2 \beta \cos{(2 x_1)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_1)} \, , \dots, \\ 
    -2 \beta \cos{(2 x_N)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_N)} \big) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
we rewrite the equation \eqref{eq_general_SLP_1} in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE}
    \big( - \mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}  \big) \boldsymbol{u} = \lambda \boldsymbol{u} \, .
\end{equation} 
where again $\boldsymbol{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)\big]^\top$ is the vector of unknowns on the corresponding grid of the implemented method.

From this formulation of the test problems, the structure of the eigenvalue computation is clear. As previously mentioned, we will use the MATLAB function \texttt{eig} for their computation. We will compare these eigenvalues across the methods, especially the high-index ones.

The differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}$ will be assembled using the following methods.

\subsection{Finite Difference Method}
\noindent
The first numerical method we consider is the Finite Difference method (FD). We use the standard second-order central Finite Difference approximation for the second derivative
\begin{equation}
    u''(x_i) \approx \frac{u(x_{i-1}) - 2 u(x_i) + u(x_{i+1}) }{h^2} = \left( \mathbb{D} \boldsymbol{u} \right)_i \, , \quad i = 1, \dots, N \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^\top$ is the vector of unknowns on the uniform grid and $h = x_{i+1}-x_i$ is the grid spacing. The second-derivative matrix $\mathbb{D}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{D} = \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{bmatrix}
    - 2 & 1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & - 2 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & - 2  & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
    \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 1 \\
    0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -2 \\ 
\end{bmatrix} \, .
\end{equation}
The implementation of this method is in Algorithm \ref{alg_1} for the Paine equation and in Algorithm \ref{alg_2} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_1} 
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \dfrac{\pi}{N + 1}$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{D}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i} = \dfrac{2}{h^2}$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{D}_{i+1,i} = -\dfrac{1}{h^2}$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1},  \, e^{x_2},  \, \dots,  \, e^{x_N} \big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$ 
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_2}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $h = \dfrac{\pi}{N + 1}$
\State $x_i = -\dfrac{\pi}{2} + i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{D}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i}   = \dfrac{2}{h^2}$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{D}_{i+1,i} = -\dfrac{1}{h^2}$  \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $q_i = \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x_i)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1,  \, q_2,  \, \dots,  \, q_N \big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Numerov's Method}
\noindent
Numerov's method (Num) is based on a higher-order Finite Difference scheme. The original formulation was proposed by B. V. Numerov in 1927 \cite{numerov1927}. In this thesis, we use a modern adaptation of Numerov's method designed for the numerical solution of Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems \cite{numerov}.

It approximates the equation \eqref{eq_general_SLP_1} by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    b_{i-1} u(x_{i-1}) + a_{i} u(x_i ) + b_{i+1} u(x_{i+1}) = \lambda h^2 \big(u(x_{i-1}) + 10 u(x_i) + u(x_{i+1}) \big) \, 
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
for all $i = 1, \dots,N$ on the uniform grid where $h = x_{i+1}-x_i$ is the grid spacing. The coefficients $a_i$ and $b_i$ are given by
\begin{subequations}\label{}
    \begin{align}
        a_i & = 24 + 10 h^2 q(x_i) \, , \label{} \\[2pt]
        b_i & = -12 + h^2 q(x_i) \, .   \label{}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
We denote the tridiagonal matrices $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$ by
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
    a_1 & b_1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    b_1 & a_2 & b_2  & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & b_2 & a_3  &  \ddots & \vdots \\
    \vdots & \vdots  &  \ddots & \ddots & b_{N-1} \\
    0 & 0 & \cdots & b_{N-1} & a_N  \\
\end{bmatrix} ,
\label{}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{B} = h^2\begin{bmatrix}
    10 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & 10 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 10 & \ddots & \vdots \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \ddots & \ddots & 1 \\
    0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 10  \\
\end{bmatrix} .
\label{}
\end{equation}
Then the whole approximation can be rewritten in the matrix form
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{A} \boldsymbol{u} = \lambda \mathbb{B} \boldsymbol{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)\big]^\top$ is the vector of unknowns on the uniform grid.

To improve the accuracy of the high-index eigenvalues obtained by Numerov's method, an asymptotic correction is applied. First, the standard Numerov's scheme yields approximate eigenvalues $\lambda_k$. These are then corrected using the formulas
\begin{equation}
s_k^2 = \sin^2\left(\frac{k h}{2}\right) \, , \quad
\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - \frac{12 s_k^2}{h^2 (3 - s_k^2)} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the corrected eigenvalue is given by
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2 \, .
\end{equation}
This correction, derived from the asymptotic expansion of the discretization error, significantly reduces the error for larger indices $k$ while preserving the simplicity of the original Numerov's approach \cite{numerov}. The same correction procedure is used for both the Paine equation and the Coffey--Evans equation.

The implementation of this method is in Algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_Paine} for the Paine equation and in Algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation. Algorithms contain the basic Numerov's method and the Numerov's method with correction.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Numerov's method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_Numerov_Paine}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \dfrac{\pi}{N + 1}$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\State $q_i = \exp(x_i)$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{A}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i} = 24 + 10 h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{A}_{i+1,i} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{B}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{B}_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$, $\mathbb{B}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12  s_k^2  /  \big(h^2  (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}}, \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}},  \dots, \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Numerov's method - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_Numerov_CE}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $h = \dfrac{\pi}{N + 1}$
\State $x_i = -\dfrac{\pi}{2} + i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $q_i = \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x_i)$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{A}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i} = 24 + 10 h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{A}_{i+1,i} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{B}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{B}_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$, $\mathbb{B}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12 s_k^2 / \big(h^2 (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}}, \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}}, \dots , \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix Method}
\noindent
The Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method (CDM) is a spectral collocation technique that approximates the derivatives of a function at specific collocation points using global polynomial interpolation. It will be implemented using the MATLAB \textit{Chebfun}\footnote{\url{https://www.chebfun.org}} package, utilizing the \texttt{chebpts} and \texttt{diffmat} functions.

Using this method, we first generate the Chebyshev points of the second kind via \texttt{chebpts} on the interval $[-1,  \, 1]$. Then, we assemble the second-order differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}$ via the \texttt{diffmat}.

For problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        u(0) = u(\pi) = & \; 0 \, , \quad \text{or} \\
        u\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = u\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = & \; 0 \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
the first and last rows and columns are removed to obtain the reduced (interior) differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$. This way, we enforce these boundary conditions. We find the Chebyshev interior points $t_{\text{int}}$ in a similar manner.

Next, we transform these collocation points from the interval $[-1,  \, 1]$ to the interval~$[0,  \, \pi]$ or $[-\frac{\pi}{2},  \, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, and multiply the matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$ by the appropriate scaling factor to get the correct Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix $\mathbb{D}$ \cite{cdm}\cite{trefethen}.

Finally, we assemble the potential matrices $\mathbb{E}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ and solve the eigenvalue problems \eqref{eq_Paine} and \eqref{eq_CE}.

The implementation of this method can be seen in Algorithms \ref{alg_5} for Paine equation and in Algorithm \ref{alg_6} for Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_5}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $\boldsymbol{t} =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}} = \mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\State $\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{int}} = \boldsymbol{t}[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \dfrac{\pi}{2} (t_{\text{int},i} + 1)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{D} = \dfrac{4}{\pi^2} \mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1},  \, e^{x_2},  \, \dots,  \, e^{x_N}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix – Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_6}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $\boldsymbol{t} =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}} = \mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$ 
\State $\boldsymbol{t}_{\text{int}} = \boldsymbol{t}[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \dfrac{\pi}{2}  t_{\text{int},i}$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{D} = \dfrac{4}{\pi^2} \mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1,  \, q_2,  \, \dots,  \, q_N \big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix Method}
\noindent
The Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM) is a spectral collocation method based on Chebyshev points. Its core strength lies in the construction of an accurate dense differentiation matrix for the second derivative.

This approximation property makes the MBCDM particularly effective for the discretization of second-order differential operators and, consequently, an outstanding tool for solving the Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

In this work, the MBCDM is implemented in MATLAB using the \textit{Chebfun} package, specifically through the \texttt{chebpts} and \texttt{diffmat} functions, which enable the efficient and reliable construction of the Chebyshev differentiation matrix on the desired interval.


The method employs the \emph{Kosloff--Tal-Ezer (KTE) mapping}
\begin{equation}
    g(s) = \frac{\arcsin(\alpha s)}{\arcsin(\alpha)} \, , \quad s \in [-1, \, 1] \, ,
\end{equation}
with the parameter $\alpha \in (0, \, 1)$ controlling the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries. As the parameter $\alpha$  approaches 1, the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries becomes more pronounced.

The physical coordinate $x$ is obtained as
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2} \big( g(s) + 1 \big)
\end{equation}
for the Paine equation on the interval $[0, \, \pi]$ or
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2}  g(s)
\end{equation}
for the Coffey--Evans equation on the symmetric interval $[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \, \frac{\pi}{2}]$. This nonlinear mapping greatly improves the method's accuracy for the two highly oscillatory test problems (the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations), particularly when computing high-index eigenvalues.

In the computational domain $s \in [-1, \, 1]$, the first and second derivatives are approximated using Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrices $\mathbb{D}_1$ and $\mathbb{D}_2$. These are transformed to the physical domain via the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx} = \frac{ds}{dx} \frac{d}{ds} \, , \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2} = \left( \frac{ds}{dx} \right)^2 \frac{d^2}{ds^2} + \frac{d^2 s}{dx^2} \frac{d}{ds} \, .
\end{equation}
After incorporating the potential term $q(x)$ as a diagonal matrix ($\mathbb{E}$ for the exponential potential in the Paine problem and $\mathbb{Q}$ for the trigonometric potential in the Coffey--Evans problem), the discrete operator takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \text{potential matrix} \, .
\end{equation}
Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed by removing the first and last rows and columns, yielding the reduced interior matrix $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}}$. The eigenvalues of $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}}$ approximate the continuous spectrum with spectral accuracy \cite{cdm}.

The implementation of this method is in Algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_Paine} for the Paine equation and in Algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_Paine}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\alpha$
\State $\boldsymbol{s} =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(\boldsymbol{s}) = \dfrac{\arcsin(\alpha \boldsymbol{s})}{\arcsin(\alpha)}$ 
\State $\boldsymbol{x} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} (g(\boldsymbol{s}) + 1)$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(\boldsymbol{s}) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha \boldsymbol{s})^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(\boldsymbol{s}) = \alpha^3 \boldsymbol{s} \big((1-\alpha^2 \boldsymbol{s}^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $\dfrac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{d\boldsymbol{s}} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} g'(\boldsymbol{s})$
\State $\dfrac{d^2 \boldsymbol{x}}{d\boldsymbol{s}^2} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} g''(\boldsymbol{s})$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D}_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $\mathbb{D}_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D} = \texttt{diag}\big((d\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s})^{-2}\big) \mathbb{D}_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s}^2)(d\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s})^{-3}\big) \mathbb{D}_1$
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, \, e^{x_2}, \, \dots, \, e^{x_{N+2}}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\State $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}} = \mathbb{A}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_CE}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$, $\alpha$
\State $\boldsymbol{s} =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(\boldsymbol{s}) = \dfrac{\arcsin(\alpha \boldsymbol{s})}{\arcsin(\alpha)}$ 
\State $\boldsymbol{x} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} g(\boldsymbol{s})$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(\boldsymbol{s}) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha \boldsymbol{s})^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(\boldsymbol{s}) = \alpha^3 \boldsymbol{s} \big((1-\alpha^2 \boldsymbol{s}^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $\dfrac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{d\boldsymbol{s}} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} g'(\boldsymbol{s})$
\State $\dfrac{d^2 \boldsymbol{x}}{d\boldsymbol{s}^2} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} g''(\boldsymbol{s})$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D}_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $\mathbb{D}_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D} = \texttt{diag}\big((d\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s})^{-2}\big) \mathbb{D}_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s}^2)(d\boldsymbol{x}/d\boldsymbol{s})^{-3}\big) \mathbb{D}_1$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N+2$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, \, q_2, \, \dots, \, q_{N+2}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\State $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}} = \mathbb{A}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation Method}
\noindent
The Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation (LGCC) method is a hybrid spectral technique that combines Legendre--Galerkin projection with Chebyshev Collocation.

It can be used to assemble an accurate differentiation matrix for the second derivative operator on Chebyshev points. This approximation property makes LGCC effective for discretizing second-order differential operators and, consequently, an excellent tool for solving Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The method is implemented in MATLAB using the \textit{Chebfun} package via the \texttt{chebfun} and \texttt{legcoeffs} functions.

The method begins by mapping the physical interval $[a, \, b]$ to the standard domain~$[-1, \, 1]$ by using a linear transformation
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x - (b+a)}{b-a} \in [-1, \, 1] \, .
\end{equation}
For the Paine problem on interval $[0, \, \pi]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x-\pi}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \frac{\pi}{2}(t+1) \, .
\end{equation}
And for the Coffey--Evans problem on interval $[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \, \frac{\pi}{2}]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} t \, .
\end{equation}
These transformations of domains happen on line 9 in both algorithms below.

The basis functions are chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(t) = L_k(t) - L_{k+2}(t) \, , \quad k = 0,1, \dots, m-1 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $L_k(t)$ denotes the $k$-th Legendre polynomial. These basis functions automatically satisfy the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The weak form of the differential eigenvalue problem is then projected onto this basis, leading to the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
    \big(\mathbb{S} + \mathbb{\widehat{M}} \big) \boldsymbol{u} = \mu \mathbb{T} \boldsymbol{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^\top$ is the vector of unknowns at the grid points.~$\mathbb{S}$ is the diagonal stiffness matrix, $\mathbb{T}$ is the penta-diagonal mass matrix, and $\mathbb{\widehat{M}}$ is the symmetrized potential matrix obtained via Chebyshev interpolation of the product~$q(t)\phi_j(t)$, followed by the extraction of the Legendre coefficients. The eigenvalues $\mu_k$ are finally rescaled for the original interval with length $\pi$ via 
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k = \dfrac{4}{\pi^2} \mu_k \, .
\end{equation}

This method is accurate for smooth potentials, thanks to the spectral convergence of the Legendre and Chebyshev expansions, while the special choice of basis ensures the exact enforcement of the Dirichlet boundary conditions \cite{legendre_galerkin}.

The implementation of this method is in Algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_Paine} for the Paine equation and in Algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation - Paine equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_Paine}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Stiffness matrix $\mathbb{S}$ (diagonal):
\State \quad $\mathbb{S}_{k, k} = 4 k + 6$ \quad for $k = 0,1, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Mass matrix $\mathbb{T}$ (penta-diagonal):
\State \quad $\mathbb{T}_{k,k} = \dfrac{2}{2 k+1} + \dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0,1, \dots, m-1$
\State \quad $\mathbb{T}_{k,k+2} = \mathbb{T}_{k+2, k} = -\dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0,1, \dots, m-3$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $q(t) = \dfrac{\pi^2}{4} \exp\left( \dfrac{\pi}{2} (t + 1) \right)$
\State \quad $\phi_k(t) = L_{k}(t) - L_{k+2}(t)$ \quad for $k = 0,1, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Matrix $\mathbb{M}$ (Galerkin projection of potential):
\State \quad for $j = 0,1, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad $f_j(t) = q(t) \phi_j(t)$
\State \quad \quad $I_{f_j} =$ \texttt{chebfun}($f_j$, $N+1$) 
\State \quad \quad \texttt{legcoeffs}($I_{f_j}$) $ \to c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$
\State \quad \quad for $i = 0,1, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad \quad $\mathbb{M}_{i,j} = \dfrac{2}{2 i+1} \, c_{i+1} - \dfrac{2}{2 i+5} \, c_{i+3} $ \quad (if $i+3 \leq N$)
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{\hat{M}} = \texttt{triu}(\mathbb{M}) + \texttt{triu}(\mathbb{M}, \, 1)^T$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathbb{S} + \mathbb{\hat{M}}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$, $\mathbb{T}$) $ \to \mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_m $
\State$\lambda_k = \dfrac{4}{\pi^2} \mu_k$ \quad for $k = 1, \dots, m$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_CE}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k = 0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine

\subsection{Discrete Sine Transform}
\noindent
In our recent paper \cite{fusi}, we proposed the use of the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) as a simple yet effective spatial discretization tool for the numerical solution of regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In that work, the potential of the DST approach was demonstrated on a single numerical example.

In the present thesis, we further develop and systematically investigate this method across a broader range of problems, including the computation of eigenvalues of regular Sturm--Liouville operators, simulations of wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, and simulations of wave propagation governed by the rate-type equations for metamaterials. Through detailed comparisons with other common spatial discretization techniques, we aim to assess the accuracy and efficiency of the DST method.

In the DST spectral domain, the second derivative operator becomes a product with a matrix that has the negative squared wave numbers on the diagonal. Altogether, for a vector $\boldsymbol{y} = [y_1, \dots, y_N ]^\top$ of interior grid values, the discrete Laplacian is realized by
\begin{equation}
    (\Delta_h \boldsymbol{y})_j = \texttt{idst}\bigl(\boldsymbol{k} \odot \texttt{dst}(\boldsymbol{y})\bigr)_j \, , \quad j = 1,\dots,N \, ,
\end{equation}
where \texttt{dst} and \texttt{idst} denote the Discrete Sine Transform and its inverse, respectively. The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication, and the vector that contains the negative squared wave numbers is in the form $\boldsymbol{k} = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2 ]^\top$.

The full discrete operator $\mathcal{L}$ is then assembled by addition
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}) = -\Delta_h \boldsymbol{y} + q(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
where it holds that $\Delta_h \boldsymbol{y} = \texttt{idst}(\boldsymbol{k} \odot \texttt{dst}(\boldsymbol{y})) = \mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y})$. For the Paine equation with the potential term operator $\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{y})$, it is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}) = - \mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) + \mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{y}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and for the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential term operator $\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{y})$
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}) = -\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) + \mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{y}) \, .
\end{equation}


The corresponding matrix representation $\mathbb{A}$ is obtained by applying the operator~$\mathcal{L}$ to the columns of the identity matrix. The eigenvalues of the resulting matrix $\mathbb{A}$ approximate the eigenvalues of the continuous problem with spectral accuracy for sufficiently smooth potentials.

The implementation of the method for the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations is described in Algorithms \ref{alg_DST_Paine} and \ref{alg_DST_CE}, respectively.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Paine equation}
\label{alg_DST_Paine}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $x_i = \dfrac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $\boldsymbol{k} = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^\top$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \texttt{idst}\big(\boldsymbol{k} \odot \texttt{dst}(\boldsymbol{y})\big)$
\State $\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \exp(\boldsymbol{x}) \odot \boldsymbol{y}$
\State $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}) = -\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) + \mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{y})$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_DST_CE}
{\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $x_i = -\dfrac{\pi}{2} + \dfrac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $\boldsymbol{k} = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^\top$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \texttt{idst}\big(\boldsymbol{k} \odot \texttt{dst}(\boldsymbol{y})\big)$
\State $\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{y}) = \big( \beta^2 \sin^2(2 \boldsymbol{x}) - 2 \beta \cos(2 \boldsymbol{x}) \big) \odot \boldsymbol{y}$
\State $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{y}) = -\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{y}) + \mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{y})$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Conclusion from Regular Sturm--Liouville Eigenvalue Test Problems Computation}
\noindent
As shown in the numerical results above, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) clearly outperforms all other methods in computing the eigenvalues of the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations. It provides the highest accuracy while remaining among the fastest approaches.

A key advantage of the DST is its spectral accuracy, which manifests itself especially on coarse grids: even for relatively small values of $N$, the method delivers precise eigenvalues. For instance, with a matrix of size $N=500$, the high-index eigenvalues computed using DST are already in excellent agreement with the reference values obtained by the Matslise software. The relative error reaches approximately $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-6}$ for the Paine equation and $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-5}$ for the Coffey--Evans equation. In contrast, the errors of the other methods for the same matrix size range from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{3}$ (Paine) and from~$10^{-3}$ to $10^{3}$ (Coffey--Evans), respectively. 

An important feature of the DST method is that accurately computing $N$ eigenvalues requires solving a discrete problem of size $N \times N$. There is no additional overhead in matrix size for the discrete problem.

For larger matrices ($N=1 \,  000$), the performance gap narrows as other spectral methods become more competitive. Nevertheless, the DST remains the most accurate overall. Table \ref{table_comparison} summarizes the accuracy of all methods, sorted from best to worst.

It should be noted that the \textit{corrected} Numerov's method (NumC) is applicable only to the computation of \textit{eigenvalues} of Sturm--Liouville problems. It cannot be used for time-dependent simulations, such as the evolution of waves governed by the classical wave equation or rate-type constitutive models for metamaterials, which are discussed in the subsequent chapters.

\section{Asymptotic Behaviour}
\noindent
Next, we want to test the asymptotic equation from the paper \cite{fix} for both the Paine and Coffey–Evans equations. That means comparing the eigenvalues from the tables above with the asymptotic curve that follows from the Theorem \ref{theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic expansion for Dirichlet boundary conditions]\label{theorem}
Let $\lambda_k$ be the k-th eigenvalue of
\begin{equation}
    u''(x) + [\lambda - p(x)]u(x) = 0 \, , \quad 0 \leq x \leq \pi \, ,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
Then 
\begin{equation}\label{fix_relation}
    \begin{split}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k & + (2\pi k)^{-1}P_1(\pi) \\[2pt]
    & + (4 \pi k^3)^{-1}\left[ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)P_2(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{\pi} \right) P_1(\pi)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)p'(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) p'(0)  \right] \\[2pt]
    & + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    P_\ell(\pi) = \int_0^\pi p(t)^\ell\,\mathrm{d}t \, .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Now we use this theorem to derive asymptotes for our two test problems. The asymptote for the Paine equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{e^{\pi} - 1}{2 \pi k} + \frac{1}{4 \pi k^3} \left( \frac{e^{2 \pi} -1}{4} - \frac{(e^{\pi}-1)^2}{\pi} + \frac{e^{\pi}}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \right) + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and the asymptote for the Coffey--Evans equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{225}{k} + \frac{38 192.75}{k^3} - \frac{50625}{k^3} + O(k^{-4}) \, .
\end{equation}

We can see the plots for both of them in the figures below. For both equations, we plotted intervals $\lambda_k \in [1, \, 500]$, $\lambda_k \in [1, \, 100]$, $\lambda_k \in [1, \, 50]$, and $\lambda_k \in [1, \, 10]$.

\subsection{Asymptotic Consistency of Discrete Sine Transform Eigenvalues}
\noindent
The reader might wonder whether the remarkable accuracy of the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) approach is merely coincidental. It is not. The accuracy stems from the fact that the eigenvalues of the discrete operator obtained via DST are asymptotically consistent with those of the continuous Sturm--Liouville problem.

Recall the asymptotic expansion for the square root of the $k$-th eigenvalue of the continuous problem \ref{theorem} through the relation \eqref{fix_relation}. Squaring this expansion and neglecting higher-order terms yields the asymptotic behaviour of $\lambda_k$ itself
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \lambda_k = k^2 & + \dfrac{P_1(\pi)}{\pi} + \dfrac{P_1(\pi)}{4 \pi^2 k^2}^2 \\[2pt] & + \dfrac{1}{2 \pi k ^2} \bigg[ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)P_2(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{\pi} \right) P_1(\pi)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)p'(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) p'(0)\bigg] \\[2pt] 
    & + O(k^{-3}) \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
As $k \to \infty$, the dominant term is clearly
\begin{equation}\label{lambda_k2}
    \lambda_k \sim k^2 \, .
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the theory of the Discrete Sine Transform tells us that the matrix representation of the negative second derivative $  -\frac{d^2}{dx^2}  $ (with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions) is diagonalized exactly in the DST basis. Specifically, if we denote by $  \boldsymbol{k} = [-1^2, -2^2, \dots, -N^2]^\top  $ the vector of eigenvalues of the discrete second-derivative operator $  \Delta_h  $, then the eigenvalues of the operator $ -\Delta_h  $ are precisely
\begin{equation}
    [1^2, \, 2^2, \, 3^2, \, \dots, \, N^2] \, .
\end{equation}
Thus, in the limit for large $k$, the discrete eigenvalues obtained by the DST approach coincide with the leading-order term of the continuous asymptotic expansion \eqref{lambda_k2}. This perfect matching of the leading asymptotics explains why the DST method reproduces the eigenvalues of the underlying differential operator with spectral accuracy for sufficiently smooth potentials, even on relatively coarse grids.



\chapter{Semi-Discrete Simulations of Classical Wave Equation}
\noindent
The focus of this chapter will be on semi-discrete simulations of the classical wave equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave}
    \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial t^2 } = c^2 \, \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial x^2} \, .
\end{equation}
For the purposes of simplification, it is assumed that $c = 1$.

Our goal will again be to compare the discretization techniques introduced in the previous chapter and determine which one is best. To compare these methods fairly, we implement them in \eqref{eq_wave} only in space and solve the resulting system exactly in time; thus, we conduct the semi-discrete simulations.

The focus of this study will be on two cases of boundary conditions: periodic boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(x, t) = u(x + I, t) \,  \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(0,  t) = u(I, t) = 0 \,  \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the interval length.

The initial waves under consideration for our simulations will be in function form
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_wave_general}
    \begin{align}
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} \, , \label{one_wave} \\[2pt]
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx - \omega_l t )} \, , \label{two_waves_same} \\[2pt]
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx + \omega_l t )} \, , \label{two_waves_oppo}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the relation between wave number $k$ and angular frequency $\omega_k$ is $\omega_k = c  k$. And analogously, for wave number $l$ and angular frequency $\omega_l$, the relation is $\omega_k = c  k$. The values $k$ and $l$ are chosen appropriately in each simulation to fit our purposes.

First, we introduce exact time evolution and then present the plots from our simulations. The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest whole number.

\section{Connection between the Sturm--Liouville Eigenvalue Problem and the Wave Equation}
\noindent
The classical wave equation \eqref{eq_wave} describes the propagation of waves in a one-dimensional homogeneous medium with constant wave speed $c$. A key property of wave phenomena is the dispersion relation, which links the temporal frequency $\omega$ to the spatial behaviour of the solution. In order to preserve the correct dispersion relation in numerical simulations, an accurate discretization of the spatial differential operator is needed.

One of the most elegant ways to expose this connection is through separation of variables. Assuming a time-harmonic solution of the form
\begin{equation}
    u(x,t) = e^{i \omega t} p(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
and substituting it into the wave equation \eqref{eq_wave} yields
\begin{equation}
    -\omega^2 p(x) = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 p(x)}{\partial x^2} \, .
\end{equation}
Rearranging this equation, we arrive at the Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problem \eqref{eq_general_test_problem} for the function $p(x)$
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
       & - \frac{\partial^2 p(x)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} p(x) \, , \\[5pt]
       & - p''(x) + q(x) p(x) = \lambda p(x) \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\lambda = \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}$ and $q(x)=0$. Consequently, the squared frequencies $\omega^2$ of the time-dependent wave problem are directly determined by the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of the corresponding spatial Sturm--Liouville operator (scaled by $c^2$).

This equivalence reveals a profound link between the two problems: any reliable numerical method for solving the Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problem can be directly applied to wave propagation simulations while accurately preserving the dispersion relation. In particular, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) approach presented in the previous chapter provides a spectrally accurate discretization of the spatial operator, ensuring that the discrete dispersion relation closely matches the exact continuous one for a broad range of frequencies.




\section{Exact Time Evolution}
\noindent
Instead of using conventional numerical time-stepping schemes, we employ exact time integration based on the eigen-decomposition of the spatial operator. This approach yields analytically exact solutions and consists of the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Discretize the spatial differential operator and obtain a matrix $\mathbb{D}$ that approximates the second derivative
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{D} \approx \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item Substitute into the wave equation \eqref{eq_wave} (with $c=1$) and obtain the semi-discrete system
    \begin{equation}\label{eq_exact_time}
        \frac{\partial^2 \boldsymbol{u}(t)}{\partial t^2} = \mathbb{D} \boldsymbol{u}(t) \, .
    \end{equation}
    where $\boldsymbol{u}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the vector of approximate solution values at the interior grid points at time $t$.

    \item Solve the eigenvalue problem
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{D} \boldsymbol{v}_k = \lambda_k \boldsymbol{v}_k \, , \qquad k=1,\dots,N \, ,
    \end{equation}
    where $\lambda_k < 0$ are the eigenvalues and $\boldsymbol{v}_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$ are the corresponding discrete eigenvectors. The associated eigenfunctions are denoted by $v_k(x)$ and the associated angular frequencies are
    \begin{equation}\label{omega}
        \omega_k = \sqrt{-\lambda_k} \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item The general solution of \eqref{eq_exact_time} in the modal basis is
    \begin{equation}
        \boldsymbol{u}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N c_k(t) \boldsymbol{v}_k,
    \end{equation}
    where each modal coefficient $c_k(t)$ satisfies the harmonic oscillator equation
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{d^2 c_k(t)}{dt^2} + \omega_k^2 c_k(t) = 0 \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item Projecting the initial conditions $u(x,0)$ and $u_t(x,0)$ onto the eigenbasis gives the modal coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$.

    \item The analytical solution of the harmonic oscillator equation is
    \begin{equation}
        c_k(t) = a_k \cos(\omega_k t) + b_k \sin(\omega_k t) \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item The full solution at any time $t$ is finally reconstructed by superposition
    \begin{equation}
        u(x,t) = \sum_{k=1}^N c_k(t) \, v_k(x) \, .
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

This approach guarantees that the time evolution is exact — there is no time-discretization error. The only source of error originates from the spatial discretization of the continuous second-order derivative operator.


\section{Methods Used in the Codes}
\noindent
The spatial matrix operator $\mathbb{A}$ is constructed using several different discretization techniques, depending on the type of boundary conditions.

\subsubsection*{Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)}
\noindent
Uses complex exponential modes $e^{i 2\pi k x / I}$ (or equivalently, real sine and cosine pairs) on a periodic interval $[0, \, I]$. The frequencies are exactly $\omega_k = |k|$. This method is designed specifically for periodic boundary conditions. 

\subsubsection*{Discrete Sine Transform (DST)}
\noindent
Uses the analytically known eigenmodes $\sin(k \pi x/I)$ on the interval $[0, \, I]$. The frequencies are exactly $\omega_k = k$. This method is designed specifically for Dirichlet boundary conditions.

\subsubsection*{FD, Num, CDM, MBCD, LGCC}
\noindent
For these methods, the exact modal time evolution, as described above, is applied. The relation for frequencies is \eqref{omega}. It is evident that the methods suitable for periodic boundary conditions are Finite Differences and Numerov's method. For the Dirichlet boundary conditions, all methods are equally applicable.

\section{Conclusion from the Semi-Discrete Simulations of Classical Wave Equation}
\noindent
A series of numerical simulations was performed to investigate the propagation of waves \eqref{eq_wave_general} governed by the classical wave equation \eqref{eq_wave}. All spatial discretization methods developed or considered in this work were employed and compared to one another.

To highlight the differences in accuracy between the individual methods, it is essential to use a relatively small number of grid points $N$. When $N$ is chosen sufficiently large, all spectral methods converge to very similar (and accurate) results, making their relative performance difficult to distinguish (at least on a short time interval). The true advantage of the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) becomes clearly visible precisely in the regime of small $N$, where DST already delivers accurate eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, while the other methods still exhibit noticeable errors.

The numerical experiments confirm that the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) provides the highest accuracy for problems with periodic boundary conditions. For Dirichlet boundary conditions, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) clearly outperforms all other methods. Among the remaining techniques, the standard Finite Difference method (FD) is the least accurate, followed by Numerov's method (Num). The Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (CDM), the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM), and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation (LGCC) method achieve significantly better accuracy than the two lowest-order approaches, yet they remain visibly less accurate than the DST, especially on coarse grids (small $N$).




\chapter{Semi-Discrete Simulations of Rate-Type Equations for Metamaterials}
\noindent
In this chapter, we will focus on simulations similar to those in the previous chapter. This time, however, we will consider a rate-type equation for the evolution of waves in a one-dimensional metamaterial. The metamaterial consists of an infinite lattice system made of cells with inner mass. Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial} illustrates the configuration of the metamaterial.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/figures_general/mass-in-mass-lattice.eps}
    \caption{Schematic figure of one-dimensional infinite lattice mass-in-mass system.}
    \label{fig_metamaterial}
\end{figure}
\noindent
The parameter $k_1$ denotes the stiffness of the springs between the outer masses,~$k_2$ signifies the stiffness of the springs between the inner and outer masses, $m_1$ and $m_2$ represent the masses of the outer and inner parts of the cell, respectively, $L$ is the length of one periodic part of the lattice, and finally, $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the displacements of the outer and inner masses, respectively.

\section{Rate-Type Equations}
\noindent
The classical Newtonian description of the system in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial} is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_newton_metamaterial}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{dt^2} + k_1 \bigl( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j+1)}\bigr) + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j-1)} \right) + k_2 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_2^{(j)} \right) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & m_2^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_2^{(j)}}{d t^2} +  k_2 \left( u_2^{(j)} - u_1^{(j)}  \right) = 0 \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
for $j = 1, \dots,N$. The rate-type equations are derived from \eqref{eq_newton_metamaterial} and their formulation is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_rate_type}
    \begin{align}
    & m_1^{(j)} \frac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{dt^2} - \sigma^{(j)} = 0 \, , 
        \label{} \\[5pt]
        &\begin{aligned}
        \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{k_2} \frac{d^2 \sigma^{(j)}}{dt^2} 
            + \left(1 + \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{m_1^{(j)}} \right) \sigma^{(j)} 
            = \frac{m_2^{(j)} k_1}{k_2} \frac{d^2 }{dt^2 } \bigl( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \bigr) \; \, \\
            \quad + k_1 \bigl( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \bigr) \, ,
        \end{aligned}
        \label{}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\sigma$ is stress/force. The standard naive identification of a corresponding spatially homogeneous continuous model is based on the approximation
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2 } \approx \dfrac{u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)}}{L^2} \, , 
\end{equation}
where, for the sake of simplicity, we omit the index in $u_1$ and set $u$. This brings us to the continuous model
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_rate_type_continuous}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{m_2}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \sigma(x,t)}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma(x,t)  = \dfrac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right) & \\ + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
The derivation of the rate-type model \eqref{eq_rate_type} from the initial Newtonian description \eqref{eq_newton_metamaterial} is done in detail in the paper \cite{cichra}. 

Next, we can rewrite the equations \eqref{eq_rate_type_continuous} in the form
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_meta_derivation}
    \begin{align}
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{1}{m_1} \sigma(x,t) \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2} \left(\sigma(x,t) - k_1 L^2  \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right)  = - k_2 \left( \dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) \sigma(x,t) & \\ + \dfrac{k_1 k_2 L^2}{m_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
And if we define a new function $a(x,t)$ through the following relation
\begin{subequations}\label{a_def}
    \begin{align}
    a(x,t) \equiv \sigma(x,t) - k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2}  \, , \\[5pt]
    \sigma(x,t) \equiv a(x,t) + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2}  \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
which can be substituted in \eqref{eq_meta_derivation}, we obtain the following equations
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_continuous_meta}
    \begin{align}
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{1}{m_1}a(x,t) \, , \\[5pt]
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 a(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = - \dfrac{k_1 k_2 L^2}{m_1} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} - k_2 \left( \dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) a(x,t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

Now, we discretize \eqref{eq_continuous_meta} by denoting the vectors
\begin{align}\label{}
    \boldsymbol{u}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        u^{(1)}(t)\\
        u^{(2)}(t)\\
        \vdots\\
        u^{(N)}(t)
    \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \sigma^{(1)}(t)\\
        \sigma^{(2)}(t)\\
        \vdots\\
        \sigma^{(N)}(t)
    \end{pmatrix} \, ,
\end{align}
where the components of the node values vector $\boldsymbol{u}(t)$ are denoted by $u^{(j)}(t)=u(x_j,t)$, with $x_j=jL$ for $j=1, \dots ,N$ and analogously for the vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)$ \cite{tozzi}. Let $\mathbb{D}$ be our differentiation matrix, which approximates the continuous second derivative operator. And lastly, define the discrete counterpart of relations \eqref{a_def} by
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{a}(t) \equiv \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t) - k_1 L^2 \mathbb{D}\boldsymbol{u}(t) \, , \\[5pt]
    \boldsymbol{ \sigma }(t) \equiv \boldsymbol{a}(t) + k_1 L^2 \mathbb{D}\boldsymbol{u}(t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
This allows us to rewrite the continuous equations \eqref{eq_continuous_meta} as a semi-discrete system of ordinary differential equations for the unknown vectors $\boldsymbol{u}(t)$ and $\boldsymbol{a}(t)$. So, we have
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_meta_step}
    \begin{align}
       & \dfrac{d^2 \boldsymbol{u}(t)}{dt^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D}\boldsymbol{u}(t) + \dfrac{1}{m _1} \boldsymbol{a}(t) \, ,\\[5pt]
       & \dfrac{d^2 \boldsymbol{a}(t)}{dt^2} = -\dfrac{k_2 k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D}\boldsymbol{u}(t)-k_2\left(\dfrac{1}{m_2}+\dfrac{1}{m_1}\right) \boldsymbol{a}(t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

If we define the matrix $\mathbb{M}$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{M} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D} & \dfrac{1}{m_1} \mathbb{I} \\
    - \dfrac{k_2 k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D} & -k_2 \left(\dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) \mathbb{I}  \\
\end{bmatrix} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times 2N}$, we can rewrite the system \eqref{eq_meta_step} in the form
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \boldsymbol{u}(t) \\
    \boldsymbol{a}(t)  \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbb{M} 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \boldsymbol{u}(t) \\
    \boldsymbol{a}(t)  \\
    \end{bmatrix} \, .
\end{equation}

In the following simulations, we use the identical exact time integration procedure that was implemented in the previous chapter. The method relies on computing the eigen-decomposition of the discretized system matrix $\mathbb{M}$. So, we solve the eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{M} \mathbb{V} = \Lambda \mathbb{V} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the columns of $\mathbb{V}$ are the eigenvectors (spatial modes), the matrix
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda = \texttt{diag} \left(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N \right) \, ,
\end{equation}
and the angular frequencies are determined by $\omega_k = \sqrt{-\lambda_k}$. Projecting the initial conditions onto the eigenbasis yields the modal coefficients. The solution at any time~$t$ is then given by the exact superposition
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{u}(t) \\[5pt]
\boldsymbol{a}(t)
\end{bmatrix}
= \mathbb{V} 
\begin{bmatrix}
\texttt{diag}(\cos(\omega_k t)) & \texttt{diag}(\omega_k^{-1} \sin(\omega_k t))
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbb{V}^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
\boldsymbol{y}_0 \\[5pt]
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}_0
\end{bmatrix} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{y}_0$ and $\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}_0$ are the initial conditions.

This approach ensures that the time integration is analytically exact; the only source of error is the spatial discretization. The matrix $\mathbb{D}$ will be chosen appropriately depending on which method we wish to implement: FD, Num, CDM, MBCDM, LGCC, DFT or DST.

The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest whole number.

\section{Dirichlet Boundary Conditions}

In this section, we will focus on homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(0, t) = u(I, t) = 0 \, \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the interval length.

Since all of our methods are suitable for Dirichlet boundary conditions, we will run simulations using each method.

\section{Conclusion from the Semi-Discrete Simulations of Rate-Type Equations for Metamaterials}
\noindent
In this chapter, a numerical analysis analogous to the one presented in the previous chapter was performed, this time using the rate-type governing equations.

As in the previous chapter, a relatively small number of grid points $N$ was deliberately chosen in order to clearly distinguish the performance of the different discretization methods. For large $N$ the differences between the spectral methods become negligible, as already discussed.

The results confirm that, for problems with periodic boundary conditions, the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) provides the best performance. Numerov's method ranks second, followed by the Finite Difference method.

In the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) again proves to be the most accurate approach. The Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (CDM), Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM), and Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation (LGCC) method achieve accuracy close to that of the DST, albeit slightly lower. As before, Numerov's method and the standard Finite Difference method remain the least accurate.

Overall, the outcomes are consistent with those observed in the previous chapter, with the DFT and DST clearly outperforming the other methods.



\chapter{Comparison of Wave Propagation Governed by Classical Wave Equation and Rate-Type Equations for Metamaterials}
\noindent
In the previous chapters, we demonstrated that the best spatial approximation for periodic boundary problems is the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Discrete Sine Transform for Dirichlet boundary problems. Now we would like to use these two approximations for comparison between the behaviour of metamaterials and classical elastic materials.

\section{Governing Equations}
\noindent
The metamaterial consisting of the lattice structure depicted in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial} has continuous rate-type governing equations
\begin{subequations}\label{continuous_meta}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{m_2}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \sigma(x,t)}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma(x,t)  = \dfrac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right) & \\ + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
If we substitute $m_2 = 0$ into \eqref{continuous_meta}, we get the equations
\begin{subequations}\label{}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \sigma(x,t)  = k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
By merging them together, we obtain
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \, , \\
    & c = \sqrt{\dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}} \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
which is a classical wave equation with its speed of propagation $c$.

In the following, we conduct simulations to demonstrate that, in the limit as~\hbox{$m_2 \to 0$}, the initial wave undergoes the same evolution for the classical wave equation as it does for the rate-type equation for metamaterials.

Depending on the boundary conditions, the simulations will be performed using either the Discrete Fourier Transform or the Discrete Sine Transform. As demonstrated in previous chapters, these two approaches are optimal for these numerical wave propagation experiments. In each simulation, blue will represent the classical wave equation and red will represent the rate-type metamaterial equation.

The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest whole number.

As the mass parameter $m_2$ approaches zero, the behaviour of the system governed by the rate-type constitutive equation converges to that of the classical wave equation. \chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}
\noindent
We investigated various methods for the spatial discretization of the second derivative operator. These methods replace the continuous differential operator with a discrete matrix representation suitable for solving both Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems and time-dependent evolution equations.

We examined two distinct classes of boundary conditions. For periodic problems, we compared the Finite Difference method (FD), Numerov's method (Num), and the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the comparison was extended to include the Finite Difference method, Numerov's method, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST), the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (CDM), the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM), and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method (LGCC).

Three main groups of numerical experiments were conducted. First, we solved two classical regular Sturm--Liouville problems — the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations — and assessed the accuracy of the computed eigenvalues. Second, we performed extensive simulations of wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, considering both single waves and superpositions. Finally, these simulations were repeated using the rate-type constitutive equation for a one-dimensional mass-in-mass metamaterial lattice.

Across all experiments, a clear hierarchy emerged. For periodic boundary conditions, the Discrete Fourier Transform consistently delivered the highest accuracy and efficiency. For homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the Discrete Sine Transform proved to be the most accurate and reliable method.

The superior performance of the DST, especially regarding high-index eigenvalue computation, can be explained theoretically. Its eigenvalues are asymptotically consistent with the analytic expansion provided by Theorem \ref{theorem} for the Sturm--Liouville problem. Since the eigenvalue problem is directly linked to the wave equation through separation of variables (via the dispersion relation $  \omega^2 = c^2 \lambda  $), the excellent approximation of eigenvalues by DST naturally translates into accurate wave propagation simulations that preserve the correct dispersion relation even on relatively coarse grids.

These two superior discretization techniques (DFT and DST) were subsequently employed in the final chapter to demonstrate a fundamental property of the metamaterial model. By systematically decreasing the internal mass parameter $  m_2  $ toward zero, we showed that the dynamical behaviour of the rate-type metamaterial system converges to that of the classical wave equation, as predicted by the theoretical limiting analysis. The numerical simulations qualitatively confirmed this convergence through direct comparison of wave profiles.

The results of this work highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate spatial discretization method. In particular, they confirm that spectral methods based on the Discrete Fourier Transform and Discrete Sine Transform offer significant advantages in both accuracy and computational efficiency for Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue computations as well as for wave propagation problems.

Future research could extend the presented approaches to two-dimensional problems or focus on the discretization of the time domain, thereby enabling even more complex simulations of wave phenomena in metamaterials and other complex materials.
