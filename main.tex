\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\noindent
The accurate numerical approximation of the second derivative operator plays a central role in the solution of many important problems in applied mathematics and physics, including Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems and the simulation of wave propagation phenomena. When solving such problems on a computer, the continuous differential operator must be replaced by a discrete matrix that approximates its action on a finite grid. The quality of this spatial discretization significantly influences both the accuracy and efficiency of the subsequent computations.

This thesis focuses on a systematic comparison of several discretization methods for the second derivative. For problems with periodic boundary conditions, we consider the classical Finite Difference method, Numerov's method, and the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For problems subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the comparison is extended to include the Finite Difference method, Numerov's method, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST), the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method.

The main objective of this work is to evaluate the performance of these methods through carefully designed numerical experiments. First, we solve two classical regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems — the Paine equation and the Coffey--Evans equation. Next, we simulate wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, considering both single waves and superpositions of waves propagating in the same and opposite directions. Finally, we perform analogous wave propagation simulations using the rate-type constitutive model describing a one-dimensional mass-in-mass metamaterial lattice.

A particular emphasis is placed on investigating the limiting behavior of the metamaterial model. By gradually decreasing the internal mass parameter $m_2$ towards zero, we aim to numerically demonstrate the convergence of the rate-type system to the classical wave equation, as suggested by the theoretical analysis.

Through this comprehensive study, we seek to identify the most suitable discretization techniques depending on the type of boundary conditions and the nature of the problem under consideration.


\chapter{Regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue test problems}
\noindent
The aim of this chapter is to introduce two regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue test problems: the Paine equation, first introduced in \cite{paine}, and the Coffey--Evans equation, first presented in \cite{pryce}.

Secondly, we will describe methods that discretize these continuous problems and enable us to solve them approximately (numerically). Our main focus will be on how to discretize the second derivative operator, which involves assembling the differentiation matrix and substituting it for the continuous derivative. We will implement these methods and compute the eigenvalues of the discretized problem using MATLAB's \texttt{eig} function.

The methods that will be introduced are: Finite Differences, Numerov's method, the Discrete Fourier Transform, the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method, and the Discrete Sine Transform. 

Our goal is to compare these methods and determine the most effective one. For this, we need some reference values. These will be calculated using the Matslise application.

\section{Test problems}
\noindent
In this section, we briefly introduce regular Sturm--Liouville test problems in the Schrödinger form. Their general formulation with potential $q(x)$ is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_general_test_problem}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + q(x) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \\[5pt]
    & u(a) = u(b) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in (a, b) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Next, let's define two specific examples.

\subsection{Paine equation}
\noindent
The Paine equation has the potential
\begin{equation}
    q(x) = e^x \, ,
\end{equation}
and is defined on the interval where
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        a & = 0 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi \ .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute this into the relation \ref{eq_general_test_problem}, we get the resulting formulation of the Paine equation
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_SL}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + e^x u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, , \\[5pt]
    & u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in (0, \pi) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}


\subsection{Coffey--Evans equation}
\noindent 
The Coffey--Evans equation has the potential
\begin{equation}
    q(x) = \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} - 2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \, ,
\end{equation}
and is defined on the interval where
\begin{equation}\label{eq_CE_coeffs}
    \begin{split}
        a & = - \pi/2 \, , \\[2pt]
        b & = \pi/2 \, .
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
If we substitute this into \ref{eq_general_test_problem}, we get the resulting formulation of the Coffey--Evans equation
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_CE}
    \begin{align}
    - & u''(x) + \big(\beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x)} - 2 \beta \cos{(2 x)} \big) u(x) = \lambda  u(x) \, , \\[5pt]
    & u(-\pi/2) = u(\pi/2) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
    & x \in (-\pi/2 , \pi/2) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Throughout the thesis, we will use the value of the parameter $\beta = 30$.


\section{Matslise application}
\noindent
As we have mentioned before, to obtain highly accurate reference eigenvalues of our test problems, a MATLAB application named Matslise has been implemented. In this thesis, it serves as the reference solver for computing benchmark eigenvalues and validating results obtained by other numerical methods.

Matslise is a MATLAB package for the numerical solution of regular and singular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems. It belongs to the class of shooting methods: it transforms the two-point boundary value problem into an initial-value problem by guessing a trial eigenvalue $\lambda$, integrating the differential equation from one boundary to the other, and iteratively adjusting $\lambda$ until the second boundary condition is satisfied. The key innovation of Matslise is that the integration step is performed using piecewise constant perturbation methods. On each small subinterval, the potential $q(x)$ is approximated by a constant value (for which the exact analytic solution is known), and higher-order perturbation correction terms are systematically added. \cite{ledoux}

This approach yields very high accuracy even for large (high-index) eigenvalues, in which we are particularly interested, with relatively few grid points. It all makes Matslise particularly efficient and robust for our test problems. \cite{matslise}



\section{Discretization methods}
\noindent
In this section, we will introduce the discretization methods that will be used for the approximation of the second derivative operators in our test problems. Later for spatial discretization in the classical wave equation and the evolution equation for metamaterials.

Our aim is to discretize the continuous operator of the second derivative by using an approximation matrix
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{D} \approx \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial  x^2} \, .
\end{equation}
These matrices will be assembled using the methods below, and then we will use MATLAB's \texttt{eig} function for the computation of eigenvalues. These eigenvalues will then be compared across the methods, especially the high-index ones.

Our goal is to rewrite the main equation \ref{eq_general_test_problem} to the form
\begin{equation}
    \big( - \mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}  \big) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
for the Paine equation, where
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}\big) \,.
\end{equation}
And in the case of the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(-2 \beta \cos{(2 x_1)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_1)} \, , \dots, \\ 
    -2 \beta \cos{(2 x_N)} + \beta^2 \sin^2{(2 x_N)} \big) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
to the form
\begin{equation}
    \big( - \mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}  \big) u(x) = \lambda u(x) \, .
\end{equation} 

The differentiation matrices $\mathbb{D}$ will be assembled using the following methods.

\subsection{Finite Difference method}
\noindent
The first numerical method on our list is the Finite Difference method (FD). We will be using the second-order central Finite Difference formula, which approximates the second derivative on a discrete grid for $h$ small enough with equation
\begin{equation}
    u''(x_i) \approx \frac{u(x_i - h) - 2 u(x_i) + u(x_i + h) }{h^2} = \big( \mathbb{D} \mathbf{u} \big)_i \, , \quad i = 1, \dots, N \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^T$ and $u(x_i+h) = u(x_{i+1})$. Matrix $\mathbb{D}$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{D} = \frac{1}{h^2} \begin{bmatrix}
    - 2 & 1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & - 2 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & - 2  & \cdots & 0 \\ 
    \vdots  & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & -2 \\ 
\end{bmatrix} \, .
\end{equation}
The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_1} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_2} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_1}  
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{D}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i} = 2 / h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{D}_{i+1,i} = -1 / h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N} \big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$ 
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Finite Difference method - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_2}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $h = \pi/(N+1)$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{D}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i}   = 2/h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{D}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{D}_{i+1,i} = -1/h^2$  \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State $q_i = \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x_i)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N \big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Numerov's method}
\noindent
Numerov's method uses higher-order Finite Differences and approximates \ref{eq_general_test_problem} with equation
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    b_{i-1} u(x_{i-1}) + a_{i} u(x_i ) + b_{i+1} u(x_{i+1}) = \lambda h^2 \big(u(x_{i-1}) + 10 u(x_i) + u(x_{i+1}) \big) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
that holds for all $i = 1, \dots,N$, where $u(x_i+h) = u(x_{i+1})$ and the coefficients $a_i$ and $b_i$ are
\begin{subequations}\label{}
    \begin{align}
        a_i & = +24 + 10 h^2 q(x_i) \, , \label{} \\
        b_i & = -12 + h^2 q(x_i) \, .   \label{}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
If we denote the tridiagonal matrices $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{B}$ by
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
    a_1 & b_1 & 0  & \cdots & 0 \\
    b_1 & a_2 & b_2  & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & b_2 & a_3  & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & a_N  \\
\end{bmatrix} ,
\label{}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{B} = h^2\begin{bmatrix}
    10 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
    1 & 10 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
    0 & 1 & 10 & \cdots & 0 \\
    \vdots & \vdots  & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 10  \\
\end{bmatrix} .
\label{}
\end{equation}
The whole approximation can be rewritten in matrix form
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{A} \mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbb{B} \mathbf{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)\big]^T$ and solved by MATLAB's \texttt{eig} function.

To improve the accuracy of the higher eigenvalues obtained by Numerov's method, an asymptotic correction is applied. First, the standard Numerov's scheme yields approximate eigenvalues $\lambda_k$. These are then corrected using the formulas
\begin{equation}
s_k^2 = \sin^2\left(\frac{k h}{2}\right) \, , \quad
\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - \frac{12 s_k^2}{h^2 (3 - s_k^2)} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the corrected eigenvalue is given by
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2 \, .
\end{equation}
This correction, derived from the asymptotic expansion of the discretization error, significantly reduces the error for larger indices $k$ while preserving the simplicity of the original Numerov's approach. The same correction procedure is used for both the Paine equation and the Coffey--Evans equation.

The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_Numerov_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation. Algorithms contain the basic Numerov's method and the Numerov's method with correction.\cite{numerov}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Numerov's method - Paine equation}
\label{alg_Numerov_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $h = \pi / (N + 1)$
\State $x_i = i h$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\State $q_i = \exp(x_i)$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots, N$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{A}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i} = 24 + 10 h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{A}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{A}_{i+1,i} = - 12 + h^2 q_i$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{B}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{B}_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$,$\mathbb{B}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12  s_k^2  /  \big(h^2  (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}} \leq \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}} \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\label{alg_Numerov_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\BlankLine
\State Assemble tridiagonal matrix $\mathbb{B}$ of size $N \times N$:
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i} = 10 h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State \quad $\mathbb{B}_{i,i+1} = \mathbb{B}_{i+1,i} = h^2$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N-1$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$,$\mathbb{B}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\BlankLine
\State Asymptotic correction:
\State \quad for $k = 1, \dots,N$:
\State \quad \quad $s_k^2 = \sin^2(k h / 2)$
\State \quad \quad $\varepsilon_k^2 = k^2 - 12 s_k^2 / \big(h^2 (3 - s_k^2) \big)$
\State \quad \quad $\lambda_k^{\text{corrected}} = \lambda_k + \varepsilon_k^2$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1^{\text{corrected}} \leq \lambda_2^{\text{corrected}} \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N^{\text{corrected}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method}
\noindent
The Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method (CDM) is a high-order spectral collocation technique that approximates the derivatives of a function at specific collocation points using global polynomial interpolation. It will be implemented using MATLAB's chebfun package, utilizing the \texttt{chebpts} and \texttt{diffmat} functions.

Using this method, we first generate the Chebyshev points of the second kind via \texttt{chebpts} on the interval $[-1, \, 1]$. Then, we assemble the second order differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}$ via the \texttt{diffmat}.

For problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions we have two cases
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        u(0) = u(\pi) = & \; 0 \, , \quad \text{and} \\
        u(-\pi/2) = u(\pi/2) = & \; 0 \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
the first and last rows and columns are removed to obtain the reduced (interior) differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$. This way, we enforce these boundary conditions.

We find the Chebyshev interior points $t_{\text{int}}$ in a similar manner. Next, we transform these points from the interval $[-1, \, 1]$ to the interval $[0, \, \pi]$ or $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$ with an appropriate scaling factor, and we also scale the matrix $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$ accordingly.

Finally, we assemble the matrices $\mathbb{E}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{A}$ and compute the eigenvalues of the matrix problem
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{A} \mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbf{u} \, , \quad \text{where} \; \, \mathbf{u} = [u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N)]^T \, ,
\end{equation}
and where the matrix $\mathbb{A}$ combines the scaled differentiation matrix $\mathbb{D}$ with the potential term $\mathbb{E}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$.\cite{cdm}\cite{trefethen}

The implementation of the method can be seen in algorithms \ref{alg_5} and \ref{alg_6}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_5}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}} = \mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \pi/2 \, (t_{\text{int},i} + 1)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{D} = (2 / \pi)^2 \mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_N}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix – Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_6}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $t =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{full}} =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2)
\State $\mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}} = \mathbb{D}_{\text{full}}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$ 
\State $t_{\text{int}} = t[2:N+1]$
\State $x_i = \pi/2 \, \big( t_{\text{int},i}\big)$ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_N \big)$
\State $\mathbb{D} = (2 / \pi)^2 \mathbb{D}_{\text{Cheb}}$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method}
\noindent
The Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix method (MBCDM) is a high-order spectral collocation technique designed for the efficient solution of Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, particularly the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations.

The method employs the \emph{Kosloff--Tal-Ezer (KTE) mapping}
\begin{equation}
    g(s) = \frac{\arcsin(\alpha s)}{\arcsin(\alpha)} \, , \quad s \in [-1,1] \, ,
\end{equation}
with the parameter $\alpha \in (0,1)$ controlling the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries. As the parameter $\alpha$  approaches 1, the clustering of collocation points near the boundaries becomes more pronounced.

The physical coordinate $x$ is obtained as
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2} \big( g(s) + 1 \big)
\end{equation}
for the interval $[0, \pi]$ (Paine problem), or
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{\pi}{2} \, g(s)
\end{equation}
for the symmetric interval $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ (Coffey--Evans problem). This nonlinear mapping significantly improves the accuracy of the method for the two highly oscillatory test problems (the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations), especially when computing high-index eigenvalues.

In the computational domain $s \in [-1,1]$, the first and second derivatives are approximated using Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrices $\mathbb{D}_1$ and $\mathbb{D}_2$. These are transformed to the physical domain via the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx} = \frac{ds}{dx} \frac{d}{ds}, \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2} = \left( \frac{ds}{dx} \right)^2 \frac{d^2}{ds^2} + \frac{d^2 s}{dx^2} \frac{d}{ds} \, .
\end{equation}
After incorporating the potential term $q(x)$ as a diagonal matrix ($\mathbb{E}$ for the exponential potential in the Paine problem and $\mathbb{Q}$ for the trigonometric potential in the Coffey--Evans problem), the discrete operator takes the form
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \text{potential matrix} \, .
\end{equation}
Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed by removing the first and last rows and columns, yielding the reduced interior matrix $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}}$. The eigenvalues of $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}}$ approximate the continuous spectrum with spectral accuracy.\cite{cdm}

The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_MBCDM_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Paine equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\alpha$
\State $s =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(s) = \arcsin(\alpha s) / \arcsin(\alpha)$ 
\State $x = (\pi/2) (g(s) + 1)$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(s) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha s)^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(s) = \alpha^3 s \big((1-\alpha^2 s^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $dx/ds = (\pi/2) g'(s)$
\State $d^2x/ds^2 = (\pi/2) g''(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D}_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $\mathbb{D}_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D} = \texttt{diag}\big((dx/ds)^{-2}\big) \mathbb{D}_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2x/ds^2)(dx/ds)^{-3}\big) \mathbb{D}_1$
\State $\mathbb{E} = \texttt{diag}\big(e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, \dots, e^{x_{N+2}}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{E}$
\State $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}} = \mathbb{A}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_MBCDM_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$, $\alpha$
\State $s =$ \texttt{chebpts}(N+2)
\State $g(s) = \arcsin(\alpha s) / \arcsin(\alpha)$ 
\State $x = (\pi/2) g(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $g'(s) = \alpha \big((1-(\alpha s)^2)^{1/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\State $g''(s) = \alpha^3 s \big((1-\alpha^2 s^2)^{3/2} \, \arcsin(\alpha) \big)^{-1}$
\BlankLine
\State $dx/ds = (\pi/2) g'(s)$
\State $d^2x/ds^2 = (\pi/2) g''(s)$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D}_1 =$ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 1)
\State $\mathbb{D}_2 = $ \texttt{diffmat}(N+2, 2) 
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{D} = \texttt{diag}\big((dx/ds)^{-2}\big) \mathbb{D}_2 - \texttt{diag}\big((d^2x/ds^2)(dx/ds)^{-3}\big) \mathbb{D}_1$
\State $q_i =  \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x_i) - 2 \beta \cos(2x_i) $ \quad for $i=1, \dots,N+2$
\State $\mathbb{Q} = \texttt{diag}\big(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_{N+2}\big)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = -\mathbb{D} + \mathbb{Q}$
\State $\mathbb{A}_{\text{int}} = \mathbb{A}[2:N+1, \, 2:N+1]$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}_{init}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_N$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\subsection{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method}
\noindent
The Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method (LGCC) combines a spectral Galerkin projection with Chebyshev collocation to solve eigenvalue problems of the form \eqref{eq_general_test_problem}.

The method begins by mapping the physical interval to the standard domain $[-1, \, 1]$ using a linear transformation
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x - (b+a)}{b-a} \in [-1, \, 1] \, ,
\end{equation}
where $[a, \, b]$ is the original interval. For Paine problem with interval $[0, \, \pi]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x-\pi}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \frac{\pi}{2}(t+1) \, .
\end{equation}
And for Coffey--Evans problem with interval $[-\pi/2, \, \pi/2]$ it is
\begin{equation}
    t = \dfrac{2x}{\pi} \quad \to \quad x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} t \, .
\end{equation}

The basis functions are chosen as
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(t) = L_k(t) - L_{k+2}(t) \, , \quad k = 0,1,\dots,m-1 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $L_k(t)$ denotes the $k$-th Legendre polynomial. These basis functions automatically satisfy the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The weak form of the differential eigenvalue problem is then projected onto this basis, leading to the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbb{S} + \mathbb{\widehat{M}})\mathbf{u} = \mu \mathbb{T} \mathbf{u} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \big[u(x_1), \, u(x_2), \, \dots, \, u(x_N) \big]^T$. $\mathbb{S}$ is the (diagonal) stiffness matrix, $\mathbb{T}$ is the (penta-diagonal) mass matrix, and $\mathbb{\widehat{M}}$ is the symmetrized potential matrix obtained via Chebyshev interpolation of the product $q(t)\phi_j(t)$, followed by the extraction of the Legendre coefficients. The eigenvalues $\mu_k$ are finally rescaled to the original problem via $\lambda_k = (2/\pi)^2 \mu_k$ (for the interval length $\pi$).

The method achieves high accuracy for smooth potentials, thanks to the spectral convergence of the Legendre and Chebyshev expansions, while the special choice of basis ensures the exact enforcement of the Dirichlet boundary conditions.\cite{legendre_galerkin}

The implementation of this method is in algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_Paine} for the Paine equation and in algorithm \ref{alg_LGCC_CE} for the Coffey--Evans equation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation - Paine equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k=0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Stiffness matrix $\mathbb{S}$ (diagonal):
\State \quad $\mathbb{S}_{k, \, k} = 4 k + 6$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Mass matrix $\mathbb{T}$ (penta-diagonal):
\State \quad $\mathbb{T}_{k,k} = \dfrac{2}{2 k+1} + \dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\State \quad $\mathbb{T}_{k, \, k+2} = \mathbb{T}_{k+2, \, k} = -\dfrac{2}{2 k+5}$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-3$
\BlankLine
\State \quad $q(t) = \dfrac{\pi^2}{4} \exp\left( \dfrac{\pi}{2} (t + 1) \right)$
\State \quad $\phi_k(t) = L_{k}(t) - L_{k+2}(t)$ \quad for $k = 0, \dots, m-1$
\BlankLine
\State Matrix $\mathbb{M}$ (Galerkin projection of potential):
\State \quad for $j = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad $f_j(t) = q(t) \phi_j(t)$
\State \quad \quad $I_{f_j} =$ \texttt{chebfun}($f_j$,$N+1$) 
\State \quad \quad \texttt{legcoeffs}($I_{f_j}$) $ \to c_0, c_1, \dots, c_N$
\State \quad \quad for $i = 0, \dots, m-1$:
\State \quad \quad \quad $\mathbb{M}_{i,j} = \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+1} \, c_{i+1} - \dfrac{2}{2 \, i+5} \, c_{i+3} $ \quad (if $i+3 \leq N$)
\BlankLine
\State $\mathbb{\hat{M}} = \texttt{triu}(\mathbb{M}) + \texttt{triu}(\mathbb{M}, \, 1)^T$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathbb{S} + \mathbb{\hat{M}}$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$,$\mathbb{T}$) $ \to \mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_m $
\State$\lambda_k = (2/ \pi)^2 \mu_k$ \quad for $k = 1, \dots, m$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N-1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_LGCC_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State $m = N - 1$
\State $k = 0,1,\dots,m-1$
\BlankLine

\subsection{Discrete Sine Transform}
\noindent
In our recent paper \cite{fusi}, we proposed the use of the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) as a simple yet highly effective spatial discretization tool for the numerical solution of regular Sturm--Liouville eigenvalue problems subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. In that work, the potential of the DST approach was demonstrated on a single numerical example.

In the present thesis, we further develop and systematically investigate this method across a broader range of problems, including the computation of eigenvalues of regular Sturm--Liouville operators, simulations of wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, and simulations of wave propagation governed by the rate-type equations for metamaterials. Through detailed comparisons with other common spatial discretization techniques, we aim to assess the accuracy and efficiency of the DST method.

In the DST spectral domain, the second derivative operator becomes a product with a matrix that has the negative squared wave numbers on the diagonal. Altogether, for a vector $\mathbf{y} = [y_1, \dots, y_N ]^T$ of interior grid values, the discrete Laplacian is realized by
\begin{equation}
    (\Delta_h \mathbf{y})_j = \mathrm{idst}\bigl(k \odot \mathrm{dst}(\mathbf{y})\bigr)_j \, , \quad j = 1,\dots,N \, ,
\end{equation}
where \texttt{dst} and \texttt{idst} denote the Discrete Sine Transform and its inverse, respectively. The symbol $\odot$ denotes element-wise (Hadamard) multiplication, and the vector that contains the negative squared wave numbers is in the form $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2 ]^T$.

The full discrete operator $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}$ is then assembled by addition
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = -\Delta_h \mathbf{y} + q(x) \, ,
\end{equation}
where it holds that $\Delta_h \mathbf{y} = \mathrm{idst}(k \odot \mathrm{dst}(\mathbf{y})) = \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$. For the Paine equation with the potential term operator $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$, it is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = - \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) + \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and for the Coffey--Evans equation with the potential term operator $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y})$
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) = -\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) + \mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{op}}(\mathbf{y}) \, .
\end{equation}


The corresponding matrix representation $\mathbb{A}$ is obtained by applying the operator $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}$ to the columns of the identity matrix. The eigenvalues of the resulting matrix $\mathbb{A}$ approximate the eigenvalues of the continuous problem with spectral accuracy for sufficiently smooth potentials.

The implementation of the method for the Paine and Coffey--Evans equations is described in Algorithms \ref{alg_DST_Paine} and \ref{alg_DST_CE}, respectively.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Paine equation}
\label{alg_DST_Paine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$
\State $x_i = \frac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^T$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathcal{D}_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $\mathcal{E}_{op}(y) = \exp(x) \odot y$
\State $\mathcal{L}_{op}(y) = -\mathcal{D}_{op}(y) + \mathcal{E}_{op}(y)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathcal{L}_{op}(\mathbb{I}_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Discrete Sine Transform - Coffey--Evans equation}
\label{alg_DST_CE}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{input} $N$, $\beta$
\State $x_i = -\pi/2 + \frac{\pi}{N+1} i$ \quad for $i = 1, \dots,N$
\State $k = [-1^2, \, -2^2, \, \dots, \, -N^2]^T$
\BlankLine
\State $\mathcal{D}_{op}(y) = \texttt{idst}(k \odot \texttt{dst}(y))$
\State $\mathcal{Q}_{op}(y) = \big( \beta^2 \sin^2(2 x) - 2 \beta \cos(2 x) \big) \odot y$
\State $\mathcal{L}_{op}(y) = -\mathcal{D}_{op}(y) + \mathcal{Q}_{op}(y)$
\State $\mathbb{A} = \mathcal{L}_{op}(\mathbb{I}_{N})$
\BlankLine
\State \texttt{eig}($\mathbb{A}$) $ \to \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \dots \leq \lambda_{N}$
\State \textbf{output} $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_{N}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Asymptotic behaviour}
\noindent
Next, we want to test the asymptotic equation from the paper \cite{fix} for both the Paine and Coffey–Evans equations. That means comparing our eigenvalues from above with the asymptotic curve that follows from the Theorem \ref{theorem}.

\begin{theorem}[Asymptotic expansion for Dirichlet boundary conditions]\label{theorem}
Let $\lambda_k$ be the k-th eigenvalue of
\begin{equation}
    u'' + [\lambda - p(x)]u = 0 \, , \quad 0 \leq x \leq \pi \, ,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    u(0) = u(\pi) = 0 \, .
\end{equation}
Then 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k & + (2\pi k)^{-1}P_1(\pi) \\[2pt]
    & + (4 \pi k^3)^{-1}\left[ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)P_2(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{\pi} \right) P_1(\pi)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)p'(\pi) - \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) p'(0)  \right] \\[2pt]
    & + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    P_\ell(\pi) = \int_0^\pi p(t)^\ell\,\mathrm{d}t \, .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Now we use this theorem to derive asymptotes for our two test problems. The asymptote for the Paine equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{e^{\pi} - 1}{2 \pi k} + \frac{1}{4 \pi k^3} \left( \frac{e^{2 \pi} -1}{4} - \frac{(e^{\pi}-1)^2}{\pi} + \frac{e^{\pi}}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \right) + O(k^{-4}) \, ,
\end{equation}
and asymptote for the Coffey--Evans equation is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^{1/2} = k + \frac{225}{k} + \frac{38 192.75}{k^3} - \frac{50625}{k^3} + O(k^{-4}) \, .
\end{equation}

\chapter{Semi-discrete simulations of classical wave equation}
\noindent
The focus of this chapter will be on semi-discrete simulations of the classical wave equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave}
    \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial t^2 } = c^2 \, \frac{\partial^2 u (x, t)}{\partial x^2} \, .
\end{equation}
For the purposes of simplification, it is assumed that $c = 1$.

Our goal will again be to compare the methods introduced in the previous chapter and determine which one is best. To compare our discretization techniques fairly, we implement them in \ref{eq_wave} only in space and solve the resulting system exactly in time; thus, we conduct the semi-discrete simulations.

The focus of this study will be on two cases of boundary conditions: periodic boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(x, t) = u(x + I, t) \,  \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
and Dirichlet boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(0,  t) = u(I, t) = 0 \,  \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the length of our interval. The waves will be in function form
\begin{equation}\label{eq_wave_general}
    u(x,t) = \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx \pm \omega_l t )} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the relation between wave number $k$ and angular frequency $\omega_k$ is $\omega_k = c  k$. And analogously, for wave number $l$ and angular frequency $\omega_l$, the relation is $\omega_k = c  k$. Three combinations of waves from \ref{eq_wave_general} will be simulated
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} \, , \label{one_wave} \\
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx - \omega_l t )} \, , \label{two_waves_same} \\
    & \sin{(kx - \omega_k t)} + \sin{(lx + \omega_l t )} \, , \label{two_waves_oppo}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
with parameters $k$ and $l$ chosen appropriately to fit our purposes.

First, we introduce exact time evolution and then present the plots from our simulations. The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest second.



\section{Exact time evolution}
\noindent
Instead of using conventional numerical time-stepping schemes (such as leapfrog or MATLAB's \texttt{ode45}), we employ exact time integration based on the eigen-decomposition of the spatial operator. This procedure consists of the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We discretize the spatial differential operator and obtain a matrix $\mathbb{D}$ that approximates the second derivative
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{D} \approx \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item Substituting into the wave equation \ref{eq_wave} (with $c=1$) yields the semi-discrete system
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{u}}{\partial t^2} = \mathbb{D} \mathbf{u} \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item We solve the eigenvalue problem
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{D} \mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k \, , \qquad k=1,\dots,N \, ,
    \end{equation}
    where $\lambda_k < 0$ are the eigenvalues and $\mathbf{v}_k$ are the corresponding eigenvectors. The associated angular frequencies are
    \begin{equation}\label{omega}
        \omega_k = \sqrt{-\lambda_k} \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item The general solution in the modal basis is
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{u}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^N c_k(t) \mathbf{v}_k,
    \end{equation}
    where each modal coefficient $c_k(t)$ satisfies the harmonic oscillator equation
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{d^2 c_k}{dt^2} + \omega_k^2 c_k = 0 \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item Projecting the initial conditions $u(x,0)$ and $u_t(x,0)$ onto the eigenbasis gives the modal coefficients $a_k$ and $b_k$.

    \item The analytical solution of the modal equation is
    \begin{equation}
        c_k(t) = a_k \cos(\omega_k t) + b_k \sin(\omega_k t) \, .
    \end{equation}

    \item The full solution at any time $t$ is finally reconstructed by superposition
    \begin{equation}
        u(x,t) = \sum_{k=1}^N c_k(t) \, v_k(x).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

This approach guaranties that the time evolution is exact — there is no time-discretization error. The only approximation (and thus the only source of error) originates from the spatial discretization of the continuous second-order derivative operator.

\section{Methods used in the codes}
\noindent
The spatial matrix operator $\mathbb{A}$ is constructed using several different discretization techniques, depending on the type of boundary conditions.

\subsubsection*{Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)}
\noindent
Uses complex exponential modes $e^{i 2\pi k x / I}$ (or equivalently, real sine and cosine pairs) on a periodic interval $[0, \, I]$. The frequencies are exactly $\omega_k = |k|$. This method is designed specifically for periodic boundary conditions. 

\subsubsection*{Discrete Sine Transform (DST)}
\noindent
Uses the analytically known eigenmodes $\sin(k \pi x/I)$ on the interval $[0, \, I]$. The frequencies are exactly $\omega_k = k$. This method is designed specifically for Dirichlet boundary conditions.

\subsubsection*{FD, Num, CDM, MBCD, LGCC}
\noindent
For these methods, the exact same modal time evolution, as described above, is applied. The relation for frequencies is \ref{omega}.

It is evident that the methods suitable for periodic boundary conditions are FD and Numerov's. For the Dirichlet boundary conditions, all methods are equally applicable.
    



\section{Periodic boundary conditions}
\noindent
In this section, we will focus on periodic boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
    u(x, t) = u(x + I, t) \,   \quad \text{for all} \; t \geq 0 \, ,
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the length of our interval.

Simulations will be done by implementing Finite Differences, Numerov's method, and the Discrete Fourier Transform. Other methods based on Chebyshev Collocation points (such as CDM, MBCDM, and LGCC) are completely unsuitable for this kind of boundary conditions.

\section{Conclusion from semi-discrete simulations of classical wave equation}
\noindent
A series of simulations have been conducted to analyze the evolution of waves in the form of equation \ref{eq_wave_general} under the influence of the classical wave equation \ref{eq_wave}. We have employed all of our approximation methods.

It has been observed that the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is most accurate for periodic boundary problems and that the Discrete Sine Transform (DST) is most accurate for Dirichlet boundary problems.

A comparison of the remaining methods reveals that the Finite Difference method is the least accurate. The next least accurate method is Numerov's method. Methods that are significantly more accurate than these two methods but less accurate than the Discrete Sine Transform include the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method.



\chapter{Semi-discrete simulations of rate-type equations for metamaterials}
\noindent
In this chapter, we will focus on simulations analogous to those in the previous chapter. However, we will now consider a rate-type equation for the evolution of waves in one-dimensional metamaterial. The metamaterial under consideration consists of an infinitesimal lattice system built from cells with inner mass. The configuration of the metamaterial is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/figures_general/mass-in-mass-lattice.eps}
    \caption{Schematic figure of one-dimensional infinite lattice mass-in-mass system.}
    \label{fig_metamaterial}
\end{figure}

The parameter $k_1$ denotes the stiffness of the spring between the outer masses, $k_2$ signifies the stiffness of the spring between the inner and outer mass, $m_1$ and $m_2$ represent the masses of the outer and inner parts of the cell, respectively, $L$ is the length of one periodic part of the lattice, and finally, $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the displacements of the outer and inner masses, respectively.

\section{Rate-type equations}
\noindent
The classical Newtonian description of the system in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial} for displacements $u_1^{(j)} = u_1^{(j)}(t)$ and $u_2^{(j)} = u_2^{(j)}(t)$ for $j = 1, \dots,N$ is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_newton_metamaterial}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{dt^2} + k_1 \bigl( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j+1)}\bigr) + k_1 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_1^{(j-1)} \right) + k_2 \left( u_1^{(j)} - u_2^{(j)} \right) = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & m_2^{(j)} \dfrac{d^2 u_2^{(j)}}{d t^2} +  k_2 \left( u_2^{(j)} - u_1^{(j)}  \right) = 0 \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
From \ref{eq_newton_metamaterial} the following rate-type equations can be derived in form
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_rate_type}
    \begin{align}
    & m_1^{(j)} \frac{d^2 u_1^{(j)}}{dt^2} - \sigma^{(j)} = 0 \, , 
        \label{} \\[5pt]
        &\begin{aligned}
        \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{k_2} \frac{d^2 \sigma^{(j)}}{dt^2} 
            + \left(1 + \frac{m_2^{(j)}}{m_1^{(j)}} \right) \sigma^{(j)} 
            = \frac{m_2^{(j)} k_1}{k_2} \frac{d^2 }{dt^2 } \bigl( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \bigr) \; \, \\
            \quad + k_1 \bigl( u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)} \bigr) \, ,
        \end{aligned}
        \label{}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\sigma$ is stress/force. The standard naive identification of a corresponding spatially homogeneous continuous model is based on the approximation
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2 } \approx \dfrac{u_1^{(j+1)} - 2 u_1^{(j)} + u_1^{(j-1)}}{L^2} \, , 
\end{equation}
where we, for the sake of simplicity, omit the index in $u_1$ and set $u$, which brings us to the continuous model
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_rate_type_continuous}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{m_2}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \sigma(x,t)}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma(x,t)  = \dfrac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right) & \\ + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
This derivation is done precisely in the paper \cite{cichra}. Next, we can  rearrange 
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_meta_derivation}
    \begin{align}
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{1}{m_1} \sigma(x,t) \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2} \left(\sigma(x,t) - k_1 L^2  \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right)  = - k_2 \left( \dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) \sigma(x,t) & \\ + \dfrac{k_1 k_2 L^2}{m_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
If we denote
\begin{subequations}\label{a_def}
    \begin{align}
    a(x,t) \equiv \sigma(x,t) - k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2}  \, , \\[5pt]
    \sigma(x,t) \equiv a(x,t) + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2}  \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
and substituting in \ref{eq_meta_derivation}, we get the following equations
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_continuous_meta}
    \begin{align}
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{1}{m_1}a(x,t) \, , \\[5pt]
        & \dfrac{\partial^2 a(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = - \dfrac{k_1 k_2 L^2}{m_1} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} - k_2 \left( \dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) a(x,t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}


Now, we discretize our problem and use the approximation matrix. First, we denote the vectors
\begin{align}\label{}
    \mathbf{u}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        u^{(1)}(t)\\
        u^{(2)}(t)\\
        \vdots\\
        u^{(N)}(t)
    \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t) =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \sigma^{(1)}(t)\\
        \sigma^{(2)}(t)\\
        \vdots\\
        \sigma^{(N)}(t)
    \end{pmatrix} \, ,
\end{align}
where the components of the node values vector $\mathbf{u}(t)$ are denoted by $u^{(j)}=u(x_j)$, with $x_j=jL$, $j=1, \dots ,N$. And analogously for the vector $\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)$. \cite{tozzi} Let $\mathbb{D}$ be a differentiation matrix, which is an approximation of the continuous operator of the second derivative. This will allow us to rewrite our continuous system \ref{eq_continuous_meta} as a semi-discrete system of ordinary differential equations for the unknown vectors $\mathbf{u}(t)$, $\mathbf{a}(t)$
\begin{subequations}\label{eq_meta_step}
    \begin{align}
       & \dfrac{d^2 \mathbf{u}(t)}{dt^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}(t) + \dfrac{1}{m _1} \mathbf{a}(t) \, ,\\[5pt]
       & \dfrac{d^2 \mathbf{a}(t)}{dt^2} = -\dfrac{k_2 k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}(t)-k_2\Biggl(\dfrac{1}{m_2}+\dfrac{1}{m_1}\Biggr) \mathbf{a}(t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the relation \ref{a_def} has the discrete form
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    \mathbf{a}(t) \equiv \boldsymbol{\sigma}(t) - k_1 L^2 \mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}(t) \, , \\[5pt]
    \boldsymbol{ \sigma }(t) \equiv \boldsymbol{a}(t) + k_1 L^2 \mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}(t) \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}

If we define the matrix $\mathbb{M}$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{M} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D} & \dfrac{1}{m_1} \mathbb{I} \\
    - \dfrac{k_2 k_1 L^2}{m_1}\mathbb{D} & -k_2 \left(\dfrac{1}{m_1} + \dfrac{1}{m_2} \right) \mathbb{I}  \\
\end{bmatrix} \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N \times 2N}$, we can rewrite the system \ref{eq_meta_step} in the following form
\begin{equation}
    \dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{u}(t) \\
    \mathbf{a}(t)  \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbb{M} 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{u}(t) \\
    \mathbf{a}(t)  \\
    \end{bmatrix} \, .
\end{equation}

The exact time evolution is obtained by computing the eigen-decomposition of the matrix $\mathbb{M}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{M} \mathbb{V} = \Lambda \mathbb{V} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the columns of $\mathbb{V}$ are the eigenvectors (spatial modes), the matrix $\Lambda = \texttt{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N)$, and the angular frequencies are determined by $\omega_k = \sqrt{-\lambda_k}$. Projecting the initial conditions onto the eigenbasis yields the modal coefficients. The solution at any time $t$ is then given by the exact superposition
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{u}(t) \\[5pt]
\mathbf{a}(t)
\end{bmatrix}
= \mathbb{V} 
\begin{bmatrix}
\texttt{diag}(\cos(\omega_k t)) & \texttt{diag}(\omega_k^{-1} \sin(\omega_k t))
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbb{V}^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{y}_0 \\[5pt]
\dot{\mathbf{y}}_0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This approach ensures that the time integration is analytically exact; the only source of error is the spatial discretization. The matrix $\mathbb{D}$ will be chosen appropriately depending on which method we wish to implement: FD, Num, CDM, MBCDM, LGCC, DFT or DST.

The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest second.

\section{Conclusion from semi-discrete simulations of rate-type equations for metamaterials}
\noindent
In this chapter, an analysis of the same style as in the previous chapter has been conducted. With the exception that we used the rate-type governing equations. The results indicate that, in the case of simulations with periodic boundary conditions, the Discrete Fourier Transform is the optimal solution. It is evident that Numerov's method has been ranked second, while Finite Differences have been placed third.

In the context of Dirichlet boundary conditions, it has been observed that the most effective approach is the Discrete Sine Transform. It is evident that the approaches of the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix, and Legendre–-Galerkin–-Chebyshev Collocation method bear a strong resemblance to DST, albeit with slightly reduced precision. It is evident that the most ineffective methods are Numerov's and Finite Differences.

The outcomes are analogous to those presented in the preceding chapter, with the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Discrete Sine Transform demonstrating superior performance.



\chapter{Comparison of behaviour of metamaterial and classical elastic material}
\noindent
In previous chapters, we have shown that the best spatial approximation is the Discrete Fourier Transform for periodic problems and the Discrete Sine Transform for Dirichlet boundary problems. Now we would like to use these two approximations for comparison between the behavior of metamaterials and classical elastic materials.

\section{Governing equations}
\noindent
The metamaterial consisting of lattice structure depicted in Figure \ref{fig_metamaterial} has the governing equations
\begin{subequations}\label{}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \begin{aligned}
            \dfrac{m_2}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \sigma(x,t)}{\partial t^2}  + \left(1 + \dfrac{m_2}{m_1} \right) \sigma(x,t)  = \dfrac{m_2 k_1}{k_2} \dfrac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2 } \left(L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \right) & \\ + k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} & \, .
        \end{aligned}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
If we set $m_2 = 0$, we get the equations
\begin{subequations}\label{}
    \begin{align}
        & m_1 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} - \sigma(x,t)  = 0 \, , \\[5pt]
        & \sigma(x,t)  = k_1 L^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \, ,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
and by merging them 
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    & \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial t^2} = \dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1} \dfrac{\partial^2 u(x,t)}{\partial x^2} \, , \\
    & c = \sqrt{\dfrac{k_1 L^2}{m_1}} \, .
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
which is a classical wave equation with its speed of propagation $c$.

In the following, we conduct simulations to demonstrate that, in the limit as $m_2 \to 0$, the initial wave undergoes the same evolution for the classical wave equation as for the rate-type equations.

The simulations will be done by implementing the Discrete Fourier Transform or the Discrete Sine Transform, depending on the boundary conditions. Because we have shown in previous chapters that these two approaches are the best for these numerical experiments. In every simulation, the blue color will represent the classical wave equation, and the red color will represent the rate-type metamaterial equation.

The simulations are conducted in dimensionless time units, and the measured time is rounded to the nearest second.

\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}
\noindent
In this thesis, we investigated various numerical methods for the spatial discretization of the second derivative operator. Specifically, we focused on techniques that replace the continuous differential operator with a discrete matrix representation, which can then be used to solve both eigenvalue problems and time-dependent evolution equations.

We examined two distinct classes of boundary conditions. For periodic problems, we compared the Finite Difference method (FD), Numerov's method (Num), and the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT). For Dirichlet boundary conditions, we extended the comparison to include the Finite Difference method, Numerov's method, the Discrete Sine Transform (DST), the Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (CDM), the Mapped Barycentric Chebyshev Differentiation Matrix (MBCDM), and the Legendre--Galerkin--Chebyshev Collocation method (LGCC).

Three main groups of numerical experiments were conducted. First, we solved two well-known regular Sturm–-Liouville eigenvalue problems — the Paine equation and the Coffey--Evans equation — and evaluated the accuracy of the computed eigenvalues. Second, we performed extensive simulations of wave propagation governed by the classical wave equation, considering both single waves and superpositions of waves propagating in the same or opposite directions. Finally, we repeated these wave propagation simulations using the rate-type constitutive equation describing a one-dimensional mass-in-mass metamaterial lattice.

Across all experiments, a clear pattern emerged. For problems with periodic boundary conditions, the Discrete Fourier Transform consistently provided the highest accuracy and efficiency. For problems with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the Discrete Sine Transform proved to be the most accurate and reliable method among all tested approaches.

These two superior discretization techniques were subsequently employed in the last Chapter to demonstrate a fundamental physical property of the metamaterial model. By systematically decreasing the internal mass parameter $m_2$ toward zero, we showed that the dynamical behavior of the rate-type metamaterial system converges to that of the classical wave equation, as predicted by the theoretical limiting analysis. The numerical simulations confirmed this convergence both qualitatively (through direct visual comparison of wave profiles) and quantitatively.

The results obtained in this work highlight the importance of choosing an appropriate spatial discretization method according to the boundary conditions of the problem. In particular, they confirm that spectral methods based on the Discrete Fourier Transform and Discrete Sine Transform offer significant advantages in accuracy and computational efficiency for wave propagation problems and Sturm–-Liouville eigenvalue computations.
